# The Stone and the Logbook: Vasco da Gama at Kappad

Kappad's fame rests on a single sentence carved into a low stone pillar a few steps back from the sand: "Vasco da Gama landed here, Kappakadavu, in the year 1498." The Kerala Department of Archaeology notified it as a protected monument in 1975. It is the reason a quiet stretch of Malabar coast is on the tourist map. It is also, on the evidence of the voyage's own logbook, in the wrong place.

## What actually happened in May 1498

The only eyewitness account of the first Portuguese voyage to India is the *Roteiro*, the anonymous journal kept aboard the fleet and translated into English by E. G. Ravenstein for the Hakluyt Society in 1898. It tells a more careful story than the plaque.

On 20 May 1498 the fleet raised the Malabar coast. The Indian pilot they had taken on at Malindi first mistook Capua — Kappad — for Calicut itself, before the error was corrected. The ships anchored about a league and a half from shore. They did not beach. Two days later, on 22 May, da Gama sent one of the *degredados*, the convicts carried precisely to be risked on first contact, ashore toward Calicut; there he was taken to two Tunisian Moors who, to the Portuguese astonishment, greeted him in Castilian and Genoese.

The fleet was then moved south to Pandarani — Pantalayani Kollam — a more sheltered monsoon anchorage. And it was there, not at Kappad, that Vasco da Gama himself first set foot on Indian soil. On Monday 28 May 1498 he went ashore with thirteen men and was carried inland in a palanquin to meet the Zamorin of Calicut.

## The gap the monument closes over

Set the two side by side. The pillar says *landed*, one clean verb, fixed to Kappad. The logbook describes a week of wary manoeuvring: an offshore anchorage, a mistaken landfall, a convict sent as a test, a move to a second anchorage, and finally a landing some distance south. Da Gama's ships were off Kappad. The man himself stepped ashore elsewhere.

Historians have said so for a long time. M. G. S. Narayanan, the historian of medieval Kerala, has argued that the landing was at Panthalayini near Koyilandy, not at Kappad, and that the popular misattribution has never been officially corrected. Even the date is unsettled. The *Roteiro* gives 20 May; Gaspar Correa's later chronicle places the arrival on 26 August 1498, a version some prefer because it sits better with the monsoon sailing calendar.

## Why Kappad kept the memory anyway

The reason the beach holds the claim is not documentary but practical. Kappad is accessible, named, and roaded, with a headland and, since 1975, a dated state monument. Pantalayani Kollam is a working coast without the same frame. Once a plaque with a year stood on the sand, the claim hardened into fact by repetition — in tourism copy, in guidebooks, on signage. The stone became its own citation.

None of this erases the event. The Portuguese fleet was demonstrably off this coast in 1498, and the voyage it belonged to opened the sea route between Europe and the Indian Ocean and reshaped four centuries of trade and empire. Camões made da Gama the hero of Portugal's 1572 national epic *Os Lusíadas* within a lifetime of the voyage. The arrival was real, and it was momentous.

## What the beach is now

What Kappad offers is rarer than a landing site: a monument that openly disagrees with its own source. You can stand on the sand, read a confident sentence in stone, and know from the fleet's logbook that the ships were at anchor offshore and the captain came ashore days later and further south. The sign and the source do not match, and the mismatch is the history worth reading here.

The living beach has kept a cleaner record than the pillar. Kappad was among India's first eight Blue Flag beaches in October 2020 and has held the certification for six straight years to November 2025. A 2007 state programme added a corniche, a park, and a restaurant. But people come for the stone, and the argument underneath it is the reason to linger.
