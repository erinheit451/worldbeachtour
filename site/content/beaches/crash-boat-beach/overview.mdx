Crash Boat Beach sits on Puerto Rico's northwest coast in Aguadilla, in Sector Crash Boat, part of Barrio Borinquen, roughly halfway between the former Ramey Air Force Base — now Rafael Hernández International Airport (BQN) — and Aguadilla's town center. The name comes from the U.S. military's air-sea rescue "crash boats," fast launches based here to search for aircrew downed near the base. The pier built to service them outlived that rescue mission by decades: Wikipedia records that local currents eventually deposited enough sand around it to make the original mooring unusable for docking, but the concrete pier itself stood and stayed in public use — until part of it collapsed in Hurricane Erin's swells on August 17, 2025.

## What's There Now

Crash Boat's pier is a long, brightly painted concrete structure — the beach's signature landmark, historically used for fishing and for jumping into deep water off a ladder at the end. Reference listings for the beach name scuba diving and snorkeling as its defining activities. But since August 17, 2025, when a section collapsed under swells from Hurricane Erin, the pier has been closed to the public: Aguadilla's mayor ordered the closure for safety, and the Puerto Rico Ports Authority, which owns the structure, is in talks with the municipality about transferring it, pending a structural assessment. No confirmed reopening date is documented — check current status before visiting. The reef flat the pier shelters gives swimmers and snorkelers calmer water than Aguadilla's more exposed north-facing beaches, though the cove still sits on an Atlantic-facing coast: wave reanalysis for 2023–2024 puts the annual mean wave height at 1.2 meters, rising to a February average of 1.55 meters and a recorded peak of 2.3 meters.

Offshore, the same reef holds a surf break rated a modest 4 out of 10 for difficulty, needing a west swell against a northeast offshore wind to work — a combination that's most reliable in January and largely absent through the summer. Hazard data for the break lists sea urchins, rip currents, and exposed rock. The beach carries no lifeguard coverage at any hour.

## The Base Behind the Name

The pier's history runs through Borinquen Field, established in September 1939 when the U.S. Army bought 3,796 acres of sugarcane land at Punta Borinquen for $1.215 million. Renamed Ramey Air Force Base in 1948, it became a Strategic Air Command installation hosting RB-36 Peacemaker and later B-52 Stratofortress bombers, on a runway built to 11,702 feet by 200 feet to handle them. The Air Force deactivated Ramey between 1971 and 1973, and the field became today's Rafael Hernández Airport — 5.5 kilometers from the beach by straight-line distance, though the actual drive is longer, routed on PR-107 and PR-459 around the airport perimeter.

Crash Boat Beach's draw isn't a manicured resort experience — there's no lifeguard, and a surf break that works maybe a third of the year. It does have food on-site: stands set up at the parking lot and by the fish market, with weekend kiosks selling alcapurrias, pinchos, and fruit frappés, plus the fishermen's Villa Pesquera on the beach; no dive operator specific to this beach is confirmed in available sourcing. What it has beyond that is a specific, documented reason for its name and its shape — a WWII-era rescue-boat pier that outlived its military mission, became the beach's landmark, and partially collapsed in Hurricane Erin's swells in August 2025.
