Crash Boat Beach sits on Puerto Rico's northwest coast in Aguadilla, in Sector Crash Boat, part of Barrio Borinquen, roughly halfway between the former Ramey Air Force Base — now Rafael Hernández International Airport (BQN) — and Aguadilla's town center. The name comes from the U.S. military's air-sea rescue "crash boats," fast launches based here to search for aircrew downed near the base. The pier built to service them didn't survive as a working dock: sea currents deposited enough sand around the pilings that the launch channel silted into a beach, and the boats had nowhere left to launch from.

## What's There Now

What remains of the pier — pilings and a walkable stub — is now a fishing spot and, more than anything else, a snorkeling platform. Reference listings for the beach name scuba diving and snorkeling as its defining activities, not boating. The reef flat sheltered by the ruined pier gives swimmers and snorkelers calmer water than Aguadilla's more exposed north-facing beaches, though the cove still sits on an Atlantic-facing coast: wave reanalysis for 2023–2024 puts the annual mean wave height at 1.2 meters, rising to a February average of 1.55 meters and a recorded peak of 2.3 meters.

Offshore, the same reef holds a surf break rated a modest 4 out of 10 for difficulty, needing a west swell against a northeast offshore wind to work — a combination that's most reliable in January and largely absent through the summer. Hazard data for the break lists sea urchins, rip currents, and exposed rock. The beach carries no lifeguard coverage at any hour.

## The Base Behind the Name

The pier's history runs through Borinquen Field, established in September 1939 when the U.S. Army bought 3,796 acres of sugarcane land at Punta Borinquen for $1.215 million. Renamed Ramey Air Force Base in 1948, it became a Strategic Air Command installation hosting RB-36 Peacemaker and later B-52 Stratofortress bombers, on a runway built to 11,702 feet by 200 feet to handle them. The Air Force deactivated Ramey between 1971 and 1973, and the field became today's Rafael Hernández Airport — 5.5 kilometers from the beach by straight-line distance, though the actual drive is longer, routed on PR-107 and PR-459 around the airport perimeter.

Crash Boat Beach's draw isn't a manicured resort experience — there's no lifeguard, no confirmed on-beach food vendor in the available sourcing, and a surf break that works maybe a third of the year. What it has is a specific, documented reason for its name and its shape: a rescue-boat pier the ocean quietly decommissioned by filling it with sand.
