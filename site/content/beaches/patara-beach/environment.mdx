# The Law That Only Half Worked

Patara's beach — 18 kilometers by Turkish Wikipedia's measure, though MEDASSET's own reports describe it as 12 km — carries more legal protection than almost any other stretch of sand on Turkey's Mediterranean coast, and that protection has spent more than three decades failing to fully hold. By Council of Ministers Decision No. 90/77 of 18 January 1990, Turkey designated Patara a Specially Protected Area, banning new construction across the beach, its dunes, the wetlands behind it, and the buried ruins of the ancient city underneath. Conservationists monitoring the site have since documented hundreds of buildings going up inside that boundary anyway.

## Why the Law Exists

In 1988, biologists surveying Mediterranean nesting beaches identified Patara's sand as one of the most important remaining nurseries for the loggerhead turtle, Caretta caretta. The finding arrived in the middle of a building boom already underway in Gelemiş, the village behind the dunes, where hotels and holiday houses were rising along the beach's edge. The Mediterranean Association to Save the Sea Turtles (MEDASSET) campaigned against the construction, working with Turkey's Society for the Protection of Nature (DHKD) and WWF. Field researchers Clare Whitmore, Gürdoğar Sarıgül, and Nergis Yazgan documented nest disturbance, litter, and vehicles crossing incubating eggs — the evidence that went into the case for protection.

## What the 1990 Order Actually Banned

The Specially Protected Area designation did more than restrict beachfront hotels. It prohibited new construction across the entire zone — beach, dunes, wetlands, and the archaeological site — and required Gelemiş's development plan to be rewritten around the restriction. Responsibility for enforcement sits with Turkey's national Specially Protected Areas directorate rather than the local municipality, a structure intended to keep the decision insulated from local development pressure. It is, on paper, one of the stronger protective orders on the Turkish coast.

The order also functions as an accidental archaeological preservation law. Beneath Patara's dunes lies the ancient city that gave the beach its name: Lycia's chief seaport and, from 196 BC, the capital of the Lycian League, important enough that Antiochus III fought to take it. The lighthouse built at the harbor mouth in AD 60, under Nero, is counted among the oldest lighthouses still identifiable anywhere — though it did not survive intact: the tsunami of 1481 toppled it, and it lay in ruins until a restoration using its original stones, completed in 2025 after nearly five years of work, re-erected it to about 26.5 meters. Nicholas of Myra, later canonized as Saint Nicholas, was born here around AD 270. Systematic excavation of the buried city did not begin until 1988 — the same year the turtle campaign started — and undeveloped sand is what has kept construction equipment away from a site archaeologists are still digging.

## Where Enforcement Fell Short

A MEDASSET assessment — first reported in 2016 and still being cited as of 2020 — counted 255 buildings in various stages of construction inside the protected zone, in addition to 27 houses already inhabited in 2015, according to the organization's reporting to the Council of Europe's Bern Convention, which has kept an open case file on Patara since the original 1988 campaign. A 2015 Bern Convention recommendation calling for stronger enforcement had, according to a 2020 MEDASSET report, still not been fully implemented — alongside continued litter and abandoned tourist facilities on-site. This was not new development racing to beat a filing deadline. It was construction happening inside a zone that had been legally closed to it for years, monitored by an international treaty body, under a national government that held direct enforcement responsibility.

## What Still Works

The honest comparison is not Patara against an ideal but Patara against the coastline immediately around it. Kalkan, about 20 kilometers away by road, is a resort town of boutique hotels and harborfront restaurants. Nothing built at that density exists inside Patara's protected zone, and no hotel chain operates directly on its sand — the 1990 order held that line even where it failed to hold others.

The nightly closure is the most visible enforcement mechanism that does work. From 1 May through the end of turtle season the beach shuts at 20:00 and reopens at 08:00 — sources disagree on the exact closing date, variously giving 15 September, 30 September, and 31 October — backed by fines Turkish authorities revise most years for inflation — reported figures in recent seasons have ranged from roughly 387,000 to 699,000 Turkish lira, reflecting that annual revision rather than one fixed penalty. Patara remains one of 21 Turkish beaches still monitored for loggerhead nesting, and Pamukkale University's Prof. Dr. Eyüp Başkale reported a record 524 nests logged in the 2023 season, up from the previous record of 434 in 2020. A single admission ticket covers both the beach and the ruins behind it, funding some of that ongoing management.

## The Turtles That Keep Coming Back

Loggerheads nest on fewer than a dozen major Mediterranean beaches, which is what made the 1988 discovery significant in the first place. The species faces pressure across the region from beachfront lighting that disorients hatchlings, boat traffic, and lost nesting habitat to development — exactly the pressures the 1990 order targeted. That the nesting count hit a record 524 in 2023, on a beach where MEDASSET was simultaneously counting unauthorized buildings, is the clearest evidence that Patara's protection is neither a clean success nor a collapsed one. The turtles still use the sand. The law that was supposed to keep it empty for them has been only partly obeyed.
