# The Deed Newport Keeps Arguing About

Gooseberry Beach sits on land that has generated at least two serious disputes over who gets to use it — one in 1880, one in 2021, more than a century apart — over the same underlying question: what does ownership of this stretch of Newport coastline actually entitle the public to?

## From Rocky Farm to two corporations

In the mid-19th century, the land that includes today's Gooseberry Beach was farmland known as Rocky Farm, owned outright by one man: John Alfred Hazard, a descendant of one of Newport's founders. Hazard died in 1880, and his will did not settle things quietly — by one historical account, it "was the subject of contention and controversy." The resolution of that contested estate sent Rocky Farm to Newport Hospital as a gift, an organization that same account describes as "entirely unprepared for its management." A hospital, in other words, inherited a working stretch of oceanfront it had no obvious use for.

What happened next shaped a large part of Newport's coastline. Hazard's original donation became central to the development of a major portion of Ocean Drive, the scenic route that still runs past the beach today, from the late 1880s until the mid-1950s. Out of the hospital's inherited land, two separate beaches eventually formed — Hazard's Beach and Gooseberry Beach — each owned and run by its own corporation, formed by local citizens rather than the hospital itself. Gooseberry's corporation is the one that still runs the beach: its shareholders have operated it since 1952, the year that functions as the club's own founding date and the source of the deed it now cites in public disputes.

## The claim, and the challenge to it

Gooseberry's leadership has been consistent about what that 1952 arrangement means. The club's president has said, on the record, that "since 1952, the shareholders of Gooseberry Beach have prided themselves on serving as stewards of this beautiful natural resource while welcoming generations of Newport families and beyond to enjoy the beach and facilities" — and specifically that the beach provides more public access than its 1952 deed with Newport Hospital actually requires. In practice, that access takes the form of seasonal parking passes sold to non-shareholders and daily passes made available to the public "depending on beach capacity."

In June 2021, that framing was tested directly. Brooke Richter, a part-time resident of Newport and Miami, retained Middletown attorney Amy Rice to pursue what was reported as a quest to make Gooseberry "essentially a public beach for Newport residents." Gooseberry's president, George Loftus, responded in a communication to Newport's city manager, restating the stewardship position rather than conceding ground. No source available to this research documents what happened after that exchange — no settlement, no court filing, no statement that the matter was dropped. The dispute simply stops being covered.

## What the disagreement was actually about

The practical friction is easy to see even without a legal resolution. Gooseberry permits no walk-ons and no drop-offs of any kind; entry requires a paid vehicle, bike, or scooter pass, and even that is capped by the beach's own sense of capacity on a given day. The corporation's argument is that this is generous relative to what a private beach could legally do. Richter's argument, as reported, was closer to the opposite: that the actual language of the 1952 deed entitled Newport residents to more than a metered, capacity-limited gate.

Rhode Island's own constitution complicates both positions. The state is the only one in the country to explicitly spell out four shoreline rights for the public — fishing, gathering seaweed, swimming access via the shore, and passage along the sea — guaranteed "under the charter and usages of this state." That is a broad, general guarantee. Gooseberry's access model is governed by something narrower and more specific: a negotiated 1952 deed between a private shareholder corporation and a hospital that no longer has any stake in the outcome. The dispute over Gooseberry was never really about whether Rhode Islanders have a right to the shore in general. It was about what one particular, decades-old private contract says, and whether the beach's own reading of it holds up. That is precisely the kind of question a fact sheet built from public reporting cannot answer on its own — nobody published the deed's text or the dispute's resolution, so neither is a claim this page can make.

## The parts that outlasted the argument

Whatever the 2021 dispute resolved or didn't, some things about Gooseberry kept operating exactly as before. The police officer the club hired to manage dangerous rideshare drop-off traffic on Ocean Drive is, per the only reporting on it, a response to a road-safety problem rather than the access dispute — cars stopping on a narrow, shoulderless road got risky enough on their own to require it. The parking fees that fund the beach's operation — $30 on weekdays, $40 on weekends and holidays, plus tax — have continued, and as of June 29, 2026, stopped taking cash altogether. Newport still has two beaches born from one contested 1880 estate, run by two separate corporations, and only one of them has publicly had its founding deed challenged in this century.
