Alappuzha Beach sits on Kerala's Arabian Sea coast in the town of Alappuzha (also spelled Alleppey), about a kilometer from the town center. It's 73.7 kilometers from Cochin International Airport (COK) in a straight line — the actual drive, via NH66, runs closer to 75–83 kilometers and takes 1.5–2 hours depending on traffic. The beach itself is a working town's shoreline, not a resort strip: dark, quartz-dominant sand, a paved promenade, and, at its center, a 387-meter iron pier that has not handled cargo since 1989.

That pier, built in 1862, is the reason the town exists in its current form. Alappuzha has no natural harbor deep enough for oceangoing ships, so 19th-century Travancore built out into the sea instead: cast-iron pillars supporting a deck that once carried three rail lines, moving coir and coconut cargo from the town's export mills straight onto ships anchored offshore. Kerala's own Maritime Board now lists the structure as dilapidated, with no port operations since 1989. The Alappuzha Lighthouse, commissioned the same year a short walk down the coast, is still a working, staffed aid to navigation — the pier's functioning opposite number.

## What to Expect

This is not a swimming beach. Wikivoyage and local guidance both flag deep water close to shore and strong currents, worse in and after the June–September monsoon, with no lifeguards posted. What draws people instead is the pier itself — walkable, photogenic, and visibly aged — plus the promenade beside Vijaya Park and Sea View Park, where vendors, frisbee and volleyball players, and an evening sunset crowd gather. The coast faces due west into the open Arabian Sea, so the light show is at dusk, not dawn.

The beach is also the town's link to Kerala's backwater tourism economy. Alappuzha's canal-and-lagoon network, the reason the town earned the 19th-century nickname "Venice of the East," extends inland from here into Vembanad Lake, and most houseboat trips into the backwaters are booked from operators near this stretch of coast.

## Most Relevant Lenses

**History & Economics** — The pier was built for one product: coir, the coconut-husk fiber that Alappuzha's first mechanized mill began processing in 1859. The industry that pier served still employs hundreds of thousands of people statewide (published estimates range from roughly 350,000 to more than 700,000), and its 20th-century workforce is also tied to the region's bloodiest labor confrontation, the October 1946 Punnapra-Vayalar uprising, put down by Travancore's army and navy under martial law a few kilometers up this coast.

**Infrastructure & Decline** — The 1862 pier's own port authority calls it dilapidated. A replacement, under the Alappuzha Heritage Project, was reported moving forward in 2023 with no confirmed completion date. Nearby, a section of the modern Alappuzha Bypass had four girders of an unfinished flyover collapse near Vijay Beach Park on 3 March 2025 — no injuries, but a reminder that the newer infrastructure here isn't finished either.

**Photography** — The pier's ironwork against the evening sky, and the promenade crowd gathering for sunset, are the beach's most consistent compositions; the light is flattest and least interesting at midday.

**Gateway to the Backwaters** — Most visitors treat the beach as a stop of a few hours before boarding a houseboat, not as a full-day destination in itself.

## Practical Context

Alappuzha Beach works as a short, honest stop: a genuinely old piece of colonial cargo infrastructure, still standing because nobody has torn it down, next to a town that built its identity on the export economy that pier once served. It isn't a place to swim, and it doesn't pretend to be a postcard tropical beach. It's a threshold — between the Arabian Sea and Kerala's inland waterways, and between a 19th-century port and whatever replaces its dying pier next.
