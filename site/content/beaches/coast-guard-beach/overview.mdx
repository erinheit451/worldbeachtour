Coast Guard Beach sits on Ocean View Drive in Eastham, Massachusetts, inside Cape Cod National Seashore, facing the open Atlantic at the edge of the Nauset Spit barrier-beach system. It is a working surf beach, used regularly by surfers, bodyboarders, and skimboarders, backed by a glacial bluff that has been retreating into the sea for as long as anyone has kept records of it. This stretch is sometimes confused with the separate, town-run Nauset Beach in neighboring Orleans — a different beach entirely.

The building most visitors photograph is the white Colonial Revival station on the bluff, and it reads as timeless maritime heritage. It isn't quite that old. The outer Cape has a genuinely long shipwreck record — more than 3,000 ships lost since the Sparrowhawk went down in 1626, which is why the Massachusetts Humane Society, founded in 1786, and later the federal Life-Saving Service built rescue stations along this coast. The original Nauset station went up in 1872 and was repaired and enlarged through 1888. But the building on the bluff today dates to 1936, built after Treasury Secretary Henry Morgenthau Jr. took shelter from a 1935 storm in the old, decaying station and personally pushed for its replacement. The station served as an active Coast Guard post until 1958, became Cape Cod National Seashore's first headquarters when the park was established in 1961, and now functions as the Seashore's residential environmental-education center — not a public museum.

The same shoreline carries a second history. Henry Beston built a small dune cottage nearby in 1925 and lived there through 1926 and 1927, an experience that became his book *The Outermost House*, published in 1928 and later cited by the National Park Service when it evaluated this coastline for park status. The cottage did not survive: the Blizzard of 1978, the same storm that destroyed four houses nearby and wrecked this beach's parking lot and changing facilities, swept it into the sea. In 2024, on the bluff above the beach, two miles north of the cottage site, erosion condemned another house, the Bartlett House, after its distance from the bluff edge collapsed from roughly 45 feet to under 13 feet in about fifteen months. The Park Service demolished it in February 2026. This is not settled history — the bluff is still actively eroding on a timescale short enough to matter to people alive today.

Access reflects that instability, and reflects the seasons. From mid-June through Labor Day, the beach's own parking lot is restricted to Eastham residents and vehicles with handicap placards; everyone else parks at Little Creek and rides a shuttle to the sand. Lifeguards are on duty from late June through Labor Day, one of only six lifeguarded beaches in the National Seashore, with a year-round emergency call box — but from Labor Day to the third week of June there are none, even as white sharks remain active into the fall. Outside summer, the parking restriction lifts and the facilities shrink to seasonal restrooms and a wheelchair ramp to the beach.

**Most Relevant Lenses:**

**History** — The 1936 station, the 1872 original it replaced, and Henry Beston's *The Outermost House* give this beach a documented, dated history rather than a vague sense of age; see the erosion lens for how that history is still unfolding.

**Environment** — This is a barrier coast the National Park Service has deliberately chosen to let move: a relocated parking lot in the 1980s, a cottage lost in 1978, and a house condemned in 2024 and demolished in 2026 are three points on the same erosion line.

**Safety** — Lifeguards are on duty late June through Labor Day, not year-round and not never; the real gap is the shoulder season, when sharks stay active and no lifeguards are on duty anywhere in the Seashore.

**Travel** — Reaching the beach itself in summer means parking at Little Creek and taking the shuttle; the beach's own lot is restricted to Eastham residents during that window.

Coast Guard Beach is a place where the landmark, the literature, and the hazard notices all trace back to the same moving bluff. It doesn't need embellishment — the documented version of its history is already the interesting one.
