# The Coast That Took the Hit: Treasure Beach's Environment After Melissa

Treasure Beach sits on a stretch of Jamaica's south coast that has recorded 19 tropical cyclones within 50 nautical miles over the past 50 years. On 28 October 2025, one of them became the strongest hurricane ever to make landfall in Jamaica, and the physical evidence — in the sand, the shoreline, and the fishing beach that the local economy runs on — is still visible nine months later.

## What the sand is made of

Predicted mineralogy for Treasure Beach puts the sand at a mixed quartz-feldspar-lithic regime: roughly 74% quartz, 2.9% feldspar, and 23.1% lithic (rock-fragment) grains, colored brown rather than the bleached white of Jamaica's north-coast tourist beaches. Calabash Bay, one of the five coves that make up Treasure Beach, carries an additional streak of magnetite — a dark, iron-rich mineral sand distinct enough to be called out by name in local descriptions of the beach. This is a geologically ordinary south-coast Caribbean beach, not a manufactured or imported-sand attraction; what happened to it in October 2025 is a story about storm energy acting on an ordinary shoreline, not about anything exotic in the sand itself.

## What Hurricane Melissa did to the shoreline

Hurricane Melissa made landfall near New Hope in Westmoreland Parish, immediately west of St. Elizabeth, at 17:25 UTC on 28 October 2025, with sustained winds of 185 mph and a central pressure of 897 millibars — its 190 mph peak intensity tied Hurricane Allen (1980) as the strongest Atlantic hurricane on record by wind speed, and its 185 mph landfall tied Hurricane Dorian (2019) and the 1935 Labor Day Hurricane as the strongest landfalling Atlantic hurricane; by landfall pressure it ranked second only to that 1935 storm. Treasure Beach was not at the landfall point, but it sat close enough to the eyewall that the storm surge stripped Calabash Bay Fishing Beach down to bare rock stone, tearing away the sand cover the working fishing beach normally sits on. Aerial surveys between Treasure Beach and Black River afterward found 80 to 90 percent of roofs destroyed. Floyd's Pelican Bar, a driftwood structure built on a sandbar a mile off Parrottee Point, was flattened for the second time in its history — Hurricane Ivan had already destroyed the original structure in 2004.

## The sand came back on its own

What followed was not engineered beach nourishment but natural recovery. By around April 2026, roughly six months after landfall, local reporting described sand naturally returning to Calabash Bay, rebuilding the beach without intervention as wave action redistributed sediment that the storm had scattered rather than permanently removed. That is consistent with a mixed quartz-feldspar-lithic beach in an active littoral system: the material was there, offshore or alongshore, and normal wave and current action rebuilt the visible beach faster than the built infrastructure around it recovered. Fishermen returning to Calabash Bay Fishing Beach in early 2026 were, by multiple accounts, still working with damaged or replacement boats months after the sand itself looked normal again — a reminder that "the beach recovered" and "the fishing economy recovered" are two different claims.

## The wetland behind the coast

St. Elizabeth Parish, which contains Treasure Beach, is also home to the Black River Great Morass, a roughly 6,075-hectare wetland designated a Ramsar site of international importance in 1998. The Morass functions as a natural buffer, absorbing floodwater and filtering sediment for the surrounding agricultural land, and shelters the American crocodile (Crocodylus acutus) along with more than 100 recorded bird species. It sits inland from Treasure Beach rather than on the beach itself, but it is part of the same parish-wide hydrology that a Category 5 hurricane's rainfall and storm surge move through — the same event that stripped Calabash Bay's sand also pushed floodwater through a parish early damage reporting described as "underwater."

## Reading the risk honestly

Treasure Beach's cyclone record — 19 storms within 50 nautical miles in 50 years, one of them now a Category 5 — is not a one-off. Hurricane Ivan hit this coast in 2004, Hurricane Beryl caused damage here in July 2024, and Hurricane Melissa followed fifteen months later at a magnitude the historical record had not previously produced for Jamaica. None of that is a reason to treat the beach as unsafe to visit; the sand has demonstrably rebuilt itself, and the community's April 2026 recovery figures back that up. It is a reason to treat "recovered" as a snapshot rather than a guarantee, on a coast where the storm record keeps getting longer.
