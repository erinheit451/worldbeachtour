# The Spreadsheet That Built a Resort

Almost every beach town on this site got its start from geology, trade routes, fishing, or colonial accident. The Hotel Zone got its start from a computer program. In 1967 the Mexican federal government gave the Bank of Mexico $2 million to find the site for a new resort, and the bank's tourism-infrastructure trust, INFRATUR, answered the question with a model, not a hunch.

## What the Government Was Actually Trying to Solve

By the mid-1960s, Mexico's beach-tourism business ran through Acapulco on the Pacific coast, a resort that had grown organically from the 1930s onward and was already showing its age and its geographic limits. Mexico wanted a Caribbean-facing counterpart, one built to pull tourists, and their dollars, from the booming US package-tour market rather than compete for the domestic and Southern California travelers Acapulco already had. INFRATUR's job was to find the single best place on the entire Mexican coastline to build it from nothing.

## The Model

INFRATUR's analysts started from resorts that were already succeeding, principally Miami Beach and Acapulco, and broke that success into numbers a computer could rank: visitor arrivals, hotel-room inventory, average temperature, average rainfall, hurricane frequency. They scored candidate stretches along roughly 10,000 kilometers of Mexican coastline against those variables and produced a ranked shortlist. Officials then flew out to inspect the finalists on the ground, checking for what the numbers couldn't capture directly, water clarity, sand color, proximity to attractions.

The site the process landed on was a narrow, largely submerged barrier island and mangrove system off a small fishing settlement called Puerto Juárez, separated from the Yucatán mainland by the Nichupté lagoon. It had the weather profile INFRATUR wanted, a barrier reef offshore that promised calmer water and diving potential, and proximity to the Maya ruins at Chichén Itzá and Tulum for cultural side-trips. It also had, bluntly, high regional poverty and no existing industry to displace or compensate. Its year-round population was three people: caretakers of a coconut plantation. There was no fresh water on the island and no road connecting it to the mainland.

## Building From Zero

Construction broke ground on January 23, 1970. INFRATUR technicians had to build everything: a highway connection to the mainland, a freshwater system, and Boulevard Kukulcán, the single road that the entire resort would eventually be strung along, with every hotel built facing either the Caribbean Sea to the east or the Nichupté Lagoon to the west because the island is barely wide enough to allow both.

Private investors were not convinced an artificial resort city on a mangrove island would draw guests, so the federal government financed the first nine hotels itself. The first hotel opened in September 1974, four and a half years after groundbreaking, the same month the surrounding district was formally chartered.

## The Model Was Right, For a While

The bet paid off fast, by the numbers INFRATUR had used to justify it. By 1981, more than $1.1 billion had gone into 48 hotels, a convention center, and a golf course. The Cancún Sheraton Hotel hosted 22 national delegations, eight industrialized and fourteen developing, that October for the North-South Summit, while the new convention center served as the event's press headquarters, an unusually early credibility test for a resort that hadn't existed a decade earlier. In 2003, that same convention center hosted the World Trade Organization's fifth Ministerial Conference, trade ministers from 146 member economies. Today the zone holds more than 190 hotels and over 35,000 rooms, and tourism accounts for about 87 percent of Quintana Roo state's economic activity, per state tourism officials. A barrier island with three residents in 1969 became the financial engine of a Mexican state within a generation.

## What the Model Didn't Have Variables For

INFRATUR's inputs, weather, hurricane frequency, tourist counts, hotel-room economics, were the numbers that predicted whether the resort would be profitable. They were not the numbers that predicted whether the specific engineered shoreline would hold. Hurricane Gilbert hit directly in 1988. Hurricane Wilma came ashore just west of the zone in October 2005 as a weakening Category 3 storm after peaking at Category 5, and sat nearly on top of the coast long enough to destroy or badly damage 110 hotels, with waves and storm surge reaching the third story of some beachfront buildings. Rebuilding the shoreline took Mexico's largest beach nourishment project to that date: roughly $19 million of sand dredged from offshore and pumped back onto the beach in 2006. Further erosion has required additional dredging since.

More recently, the model's blind spot has been biological rather than meteorological. The Great Atlantic Sargassum Belt did not become a recurring, beach-burying problem until 2015, decades after the site was chosen, and it has gotten worse rather than better: Quintana Roo collected more than 76,800 tonnes of sargassum by mid-September 2025, more than 87,000 tonnes for the full season, roughly triple the tonnage collected in all of 2024. A model built on 1960s data from Miami and Acapulco had no way to anticipate a seaweed bloom that would only become a recurring nuisance forty-five years later.

## The Legacy in the Layout

Everything about how the Hotel Zone functions today traces back to that 1969 decision. The narrow footprint, one road, is why traffic on Boulevard Kukulcán backs up the entire zone at once when there's an accident or a storm closure. The total reliance on engineered and periodically re-nourished sand is why a single storm season can force a multimillion-dollar rebuild. The zone's split identity, resort infrastructure of extraordinary density on a barrier island that was, fifty-some years ago, essentially uninhabited, is the direct, visible result of a government trust that ran the numbers, found a promising unoccupied stretch of coast, and built a city there on a fixed timeline. It is one of the few beach resorts in the world you could point to on a spreadsheet before you could point to it on a map.
