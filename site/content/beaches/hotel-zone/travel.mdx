# Hotel Zone Travel Guide

The Hotel Zone is a 22.5-kilometer barrier island strung along one road, Boulevard Kukulcán, between the Caribbean Sea and the Nichupté Lagoon in Quintana Roo, Mexico. It was built from an engineering study, not organic growth, starting in 1970 on a barrier island that had three year-round residents the year before. That history shapes how you move through it today: everything hangs off a single road, and every hotel faces either the open sea or the calmer lagoon.

## Getting There

<DataCard label="Nearest Airport" value="Cancún International (CUN)" unit="16.8 km straight-line" />

**By air.** Cancún International Airport (CUN) is the region's main gateway. This page's underlying data puts it 16.8 km from the Hotel Zone in a straight line; that is not the same as the road distance. Multiple route-planning sources put the actual drive at roughly 20-25 km, 20-40 minutes depending on traffic and which kilometer marker of Boulevard Kukulcán you're headed to, via Highway 307 and then north onto the island. Hotels toward the northern end of the zone (closer to km 20) add time over ones near the southern entrance (km 5).

**Getting into the zone.** Highway 307 connects the airport to the mainland approach; Boulevard Kukulcán is the only road onto and along the island itself, so however you arrive, you funnel through the same corridor as every other visitor.

**To Isla Mujeres.** This page's data lists Isla Mujeres as the nearest named settlement, 10.9 km away in a straight line across open water. In practice you reach it by ferry, not by land: crossings take about 20-30 minutes, either from Puerto Juárez on the mainland (the cheaper, more frequent option) or from a Hotel Zone ferry terminal near km 4.5 (pricier, less frequent).

## Best Time to Visit

**Dry season, December-April.** The most reliable weather window, with lower rainfall and the least sargassum. This is also peak season for crowds and prices.

**Hurricane season, June-November.** Quintana Roo sits in an active tropical-cyclone corridor; this page's underlying data records 26 cyclone passes within 50 km of the coast over the last 50 years, including storms that reached Category 5 strength. Hurricane Wilma, which came ashore just west of the zone in October 2005, destroyed or damaged 110 hotels; Hurricane Gilbert did comparable damage in September 1988. Neither storm was a fluke of an unlucky season, the exposure is structural.

**Sargassum, roughly April-October, peaking May-August.** Since 2015, Atlantic sargassum seaweed has washed onto these beaches most summers, and the trend has been getting worse, not better. Quintana Roo collected more than 76,800 tonnes of it by mid-September 2025, more than 87,000 tonnes for the full season, roughly triple 2024's collection, and in May 2025, 28 of 100 monitored state beaches were on red alert for seaweed volume at the same time. Some hotels actively clear their own frontage; public beaches are less consistently managed. Check conditions close to your travel dates rather than relying on a seasonal generalization, sargassum volume varies day to day.

**Certifications.** Cancún collectively held 49 Blue Flag distinctions for the 2025-2026 season from the Foundation for Environmental Education, a water-quality and beach-management certification covering 7 public beaches, 4 hotels, and 38 tour vessels; that's not a blanket guarantee for every stretch of the Hotel Zone.

## Where to Stay

The Hotel Zone itself is essentially wall-to-wall resort and hotel development for its full 22.5 km, split by kilometer marker into a northern cluster near Punta Cancún (older properties, closest to the nightlife district and the Isla Mujeres ferry), a dense central strip (km 9-14, the most hotels and the convention/retail core around La Isla Shopping Village), and a quieter southern stretch near Playa Delfines (km 17.5-19, closer to the El Rey ruins). Downtown Cancún (Centro), a few kilometers inland across the lagoon, has non-resort lodging and is where most of the city's non-tourist restaurants and markets, including Mercado 28, are located.

## Safety

**Water conditions.** Playa Delfines, the zone's largest free public beach, accounts for more than half of all beach rescues in Cancún, driven by strong rip currents; red and black flag warnings are posted there more often than at most other public beaches in the zone, and swimmers have drowned there despite active warnings. Respect the flag system at every beach: currents vary by location and by day.

**Storms.** Hurricane season runs June through November; if you're traveling in that window, monitor forecasts and understand that evacuation, if needed, funnels through the single road running the island.

**Regional crime advisory.** The U.S. State Department's most recent update, May 29, 2026, kept Quintana Roo at Level 2, "exercise increased caution," continuing risk-indicator tags for crime and kidnapping tied to organized-crime activity elsewhere in the state. The advisory does not single out the Hotel Zone as more dangerous than the rest of the state; it reflects Quintana Roo conditions broadly.

## Local Notes

**Public access is guaranteed by law.** Mexico's federal maritime-land zone rule keeps every beach, including ones fronting all-inclusive resorts, open to the public up to the high-tide line. You don't need a hotel booking to use the sand, only to use a specific resort's private amenities.

**El Rey ruins** sit near km 19, a short walk from Playa Delfines: 47 Postclassic Maya structures, entry roughly 75-100 MXN (free on Sundays), typically a 30-60 minute visit, and home to a resident population of iguanas.

**One road, one bottleneck.** Boulevard Kukulcán is the only route the length of the island; traffic incidents, construction, or storm closures on it affect movement for the entire zone simultaneously. Budget extra time during peak season or bad weather.
