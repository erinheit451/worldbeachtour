Alona Beach is a stretch of white sand on the southern coast of Panglao Island, Bohol Province, Central Visayas, part of barangay Tawala. Estimates of its length vary by source — Wikipedia calls it under a kilometer, Alona Divers' own site describes a 1.5-kilometer restaurant-and-dive-shop strip behind it — so the honest range is roughly 800 meters to a kilometer of sand fronting a longer built-up frontage. The sand tests quartz-dominant, 91.7% quartz by the GloPrSM global sand model, which is why it reads bright white rather than the tan or gray of a river-fed beach.

The beach did not have that name, or any name, before 1973. That year a film crew shooting the Fernando Poe Jr. action picture *Esteban*, directed by Celso Ad. Castillo, used an unmarked white-sand beach in Tawala as a location, with other scenes filmed at the 18th-century Baclayon Church nearby. When crew and locals needed something to call the beach, they settled on the name of FPJ's co-star, Alona Alegre, who was around the set during filming. The name was invented on the spot and never let go — see the [history spoke](/beaches/alona-beach/history) for the full account and its sourcing.

Diving is what turned that accidental name into an economy. Seaquest Dive Center, which opened its first Philippine location at Moalboal, Cebu in 1981, added a second outpost on Alona Beach in 1990. Alona Divers followed in 1996. Both still operate out of Tawala, running boats to Balicasag Island, about 6 km southwest, a 20-30 minute boat ride away, where a community-established no-take marine sanctuary has stood since 1985.

Access accelerated after Bohol-Panglao International Airport opened on Panglao's north shore on 27-28 November 2018, replacing the old Tagbilaran airstrip and cutting the trip from Alona to an arrivals hall to 2.8 kilometers. Resorts followed: Henann Resort Alona Beach, the largest single property on the strip at 6.5 hectares and roughly 400 rooms, had already opened in June 2015, and smaller hotels and dive shops packed in around both in the years after.

That density has a cost. In October–November 2018, DENR testing recorded fecal coliform at Alona Beach at 16,000 most probable number per 100 milliliters against a swimming standard of 100 MPN — a two-day swimming ban followed, then lifted after a follow-up reading of 33 MPN. The same period, the national government ordered resorts to tear down seawalls built inside the 20-meter shore easement; demolition started in April 2019, moved slowly because engineers worried it would worsen erosion, and by November 2022 the DENR reversed course and approved new ripraps because the erosion had, in fact, gotten worse without them. A Manila Times columnist who revisited in February 2026, three years after a prior trip, described the water as turbid and thick with algae and the strip as overdeveloped, overcrowded, and overpriced, with only the section fronting Henann Resort holding up comparatively well.

None of that has slowed the boats leaving for Balicasag each morning, or closed the dive shops built on a name a movie crew improvised in an afternoon in 1973. Alona Beach today is simultaneously a working dive hub, a crowded resort strip, and an open argument about whether its own growth is wearing it out.
