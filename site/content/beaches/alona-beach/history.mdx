# The Beach a Movie Crew Named by Accident

Alona Beach carries a person's name, not a place-name. That is unusual enough among the world's beaches to be worth explaining, and the explanation runs through a 1973 Filipino action film, an actress who is not otherwise remembered for anything that happened on this stretch of sand, and five decades of an industry that grew up around a word nobody planned to make permanent.

## An unmarked beach in Tawala

Before 1973, the strip of white sand on the southern coast of Panglao Island now called Alona Beach had no name that appears in any surviving record. It sat in barangay Tawala, a coastal community with no resorts, no dive shops, and — as far as available sources indicate — no particular local designation distinguishing it from any other stretch of the province's coastline. Bohol in the early 1970s was not a tourism destination in the way it is now; the province's profile rested more on the Chocolate Hills and its Spanish-colonial churches than on its beaches.

That changed, indirectly, when a film crew arrived to shoot *Esteban*, a Fernando Poe Jr. action picture directed by Celso Ad. Castillo. FPJ — full name Ronald Allan Kelley Poe, and at the time one of Philippine cinema's most bankable leading men — played a man blinded by an injury inflicted by bandits led by Max Alvarado's character. Part of the production used the 18th-century Baclayon Church, a coral-stone building completed in 1727 about 15 kilometers from Panglao, as a location tied to the story's sanctuary. Another part of the shoot used the unnamed Tawala beach.

## The naming

FPJ's co-star was Alona Alegre, born Maria Lourdes Salvador on 1 January 1948, daughter of comedian-actor Lou Salvador Sr. and LVN actress Inday Jalandoni, and sister of actor Philip Salvador. She had been a child star since 1955 and by 1973 was an established leading lady, later remembered in obituaries as one of Philippine cinema's recognized sex symbols of the era.

Sources describing what happened next agree on the mechanism but diverge on the specific detail. Wikipedia's article on the beach states that Alegre "ran naked along the beach" during filming. An earlier and more detailed account — a 2013 blog post drawing on regional reporting — describes her more conservatively, saying she was "frolicking and cavorting in a stretch of a virginal white sandy beach in Tawala, Panglao, Bohol" during the production, without describing a nude scene. Neither account cites a contemporary 1973 news source; both are later retellings, and no primary reporting from the year of filming has surfaced in available research. This deep-dive treats the more specific "ran naked" claim as unverified and reports only what multiple independent sources agree on: Alegre was present and visibly using the beach during the shoot.

What isn't in dispute is what happened when someone — the crew, or visitors who came afterward — asked what the beach was called. There was no name to give. According to the blog account, locals "readily chorused 'Alona Beach'" on request, reaching for the most memorable feature of the location at that moment: the actress who had been there. The name stuck immediately, with no institutional process behind it — no tourism board, no naming committee, no signage authority. It was the linguistic equivalent of a nickname that outlives the joke that produced it.

## From film location to dive economy

The name predates the tourism economy built on top of it by more than a decade. The first documented commercial anchor came from diving, not lodging: Seaquest Dive Center, which had opened its original Philippine location at Panagsama Beach in Moalboal, Cebu, in 1981, expanded to Alona Beach in 1990, and describes itself today as the area's longest-established dive operator. Alona Divers, a Filipino-owned PADI dive resort, followed in 1996. Both are still operating under the names they opened with, still running boats to Balicasag Island, where a community-established no-take marine sanctuary — created through a Silliman University marine-conservation program — has stood since 1985.

Hospitality investment followed diving, not the other way around. The largest single resort on the beach, Henann Resort Alona Beach, did not open until June 2015 — more than two decades after Seaquest's arrival — with roughly 400 rooms across 6.5 hectares. Bohol-Panglao International Airport, which replaced the old Tagbilaran city airstrip and cut travel time from Alona to an arrivals hall to 2.8 kilometers, opened commercially on 27-28 November 2018, well after the dive infrastructure and the first wave of resorts were already established. The sequence matters: Alona Beach's economy was built by dive operators and mid-size hotels betting on a place before the infrastructure made it easy to reach, not by an airport creating demand from nothing.

## What the name is worth, and to whom

None of that value flows back to its source. Alona Alegre died on 9 December 2018 — three weeks after the new airport opened, as it happens — at age 70, of asthma and cardiac arrest, according to her obituary in PEP.ph. There is no record in available sources of any royalty, licensing arrangement, plaque tied to commercial revenue, or formal civic recognition connecting her estate to the beach or the businesses operating under variations of her name. Bohol province recorded more than a million visitor arrivals in multiple recent years, per provincial tourism figures reported by Rappler; essentially none of that economic activity is contractually tied to the 1973 naming event that made "Alona Beach" a marketable term in the first place.

This is not a story of exploitation in any legal sense — nobody trademarked her name against her wishes, and there's no indication she ever objected to the beach carrying it. It's a story about how casually valuable place-branding can be created, and how completely disconnected that value can become from its origin once decades pass and the businesses using the name were founded by people with no direct connection to the naming event.

## The parts of the story that don't fit the postcard

The same beach that inherited a free, memorable name has also generated a documented list of problems that have nothing to do with 1973. In October–November 2018, DENR testing recorded fecal coliform at 16,000 MPN per 100 mL — 160 times the swimming standard of 100 MPN — triggering a two-day interagency swimming ban that lifted after a follow-up reading of 33 MPN. The same year, the national government, acting in the aftermath of the 2018 Boracay closure, ordered resort owners along Alona to demolish seawalls and riprap built inside the 20-meter shore easement. Compliance didn't begin until April 2019, moved slowly because engineers worried fast removal would worsen erosion, and by November 2022 the DENR reversed itself and authorized new ripraps because the erosion had, in fact, worsened without the old seawalls. A Manila Times opinion column published in February 2026, written by a columnist revisiting after a three-year gap, described the water as turbid and thick with algae and the strip as overdeveloped, overcrowded, and overpriced — with the section fronting Henann Resort the one part still holding up.

None of that history changes the naming story. It sits next to it. Alona Beach is simultaneously a place with an unusually well-documented, almost whimsical origin — a name improvised on request during a movie shoot — and a place with an unusually well-documented record of the ordinary costs of unmanaged tourism growth: contaminated water, contested shoreline engineering, and a press account describing real decline. Both are true at the same time, and neither cancels the other out.
