# Sunset Beach's Memorials and Gatherings

Sunset Beach carries more organized memory and more organized crowds than its quiet daytime feel suggests. Its most famous story — a barge that washed ashore in 2021 and took a year and $2.4 million to remove — is told in full on the [main Sunset Beach guide](/beaches/sunset-beach-16). This page covers what the beach means the rest of the year: a memorial to an epidemic, a decades-old cannabis rally, and a fireworks tradition now in flux.

## The AIDS Memorial

The Sunset Beach AIDS Memorial is a ribbon of steel panels installed as a gift to the city and unveiled in December 2004, the work of artist Bruce Wilson. The design was chosen from more than 70 submissions to a public call for proposals; the city took ten years to raise the funds needed to build it. By Xtra Magazine's 2005 account, the entire process — from proposal to unveiling — took eight years, "mired from the outset in bureaucratic red tape and a lengthy public process sometimes fuelled by homophobia." A monument honoring people who died of AIDS had to survive a public process shaped, in part, by the same prejudice that shaped the epidemic's early years.

Even the basic facts of the finished memorial are contested. The City of Vancouver's Public Art Registry describes 20 steel panels engraved with "nearly 800" names. A 2010 account on aidsmemorial.info agrees on 20 panels but puts the tally at 996 names. Xtra Magazine's 2005 report, closer to the memorial's completion, describes only 16 panels and says there was room for 200 more names on two panels left empty at the time. None of these sources is obviously wrong — the memorial has had names added to it over the years, which would explain a rising count, though it does not explain the panel-count discrepancy. What's clear is that a memorial built specifically to keep an accurate public record of the dead has, itself, been recorded inconsistently by the institutions and observers documenting it.

The memorial's final panel carries a line from Dr. Peter, a Vancouver physician and AIDS activist who died of the disease in 1992: "the energy that is me will not be lost." As of 2005, an estimated five to seven million people a year walked the seawall past it — making the memorial one of the most-passed, least-discussed landmarks in the city, seen constantly by joggers and commuters who may not stop to read a single name.

## The unofficial April 20 gathering

For most of the late 2010s, Sunset Beach hosted an unofficial 4/20 cannabis rally every April 20 — unofficial in the sense that no permit or formal organizer stood behind it, just an accumulated tradition of people showing up. At its peak, in 2017, an estimated 40,000 people attended.

The event has not stayed purely informal. By 2025, the city was stationing park rangers, VPD officers, and city protective services at both Sunset Beach Park and nearby Thornton Park specifically for the gathering's public safety, and the Sunset Beach parking lot closed from Saturday evening through the Sunday of the event, with vehicle access restricted until Easter Monday. A rally that started as a spontaneous, permit-free gathering now arrives every year with a policing plan and a traffic plan attached — an unofficial event the city has learned to plan around rather than ignore.

## The fireworks, interrupted

Sunset Beach's other recurring draw is Vancouver's summer fireworks display over English Bay, for which the beach is one of the most popular viewing points in the city. For years that meant the Honda Celebration of Light, a multi-night fireworks competition. That tradition broke in 2025: organizers could not secure the government and private-sector funding needed to cover a shortfall and rising costs, and the 2026 Honda Celebration of Light was cancelled outright.

In its place, the city ran a single-night event called Summer Lights in English Bay, on July 31, 2026 — one 25-minute show, launched at the traditional 10 p.m. start time from a barge positioned in the middle of the bay, rather than the usual three nights of competing displays. Sunset Beach Park remained one of the recommended viewing spots, alongside English Bay Beach, the Stanley Park seawall, Vanier Park, and Kitsilano Beach across the water. Whether Summer Lights becomes the new permanent format or the Celebration of Light returns in some funded form is not something this record can answer; what's confirmed is only that 2026 was a one-night, cost-driven departure from the multi-night tradition Vancouverites had watched from this beach for years.

## What connects these three

A memorial, a rally, and a fireworks show don't obviously belong on the same beach, but each is a version of the same pattern: something informal or urgent that the city eventually had to build process around. The memorial took a decade to fund and, by one account, eight years in all to formalize grief that community members already felt. The 4/20 rally went from a spontaneous gathering to a policed, parking-managed annual event without ever becoming an officially sanctioned festival. And the fireworks show that once ran three nights a year on private and public funding was cut to one night when that funding model broke. Sunset Beach is, in each case, the fixed point — the same strip of English Bay sand — around which the city's actual capacity to organize, fund, and formalize things has visibly shifted.
