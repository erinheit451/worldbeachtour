# The Rock They Blew Apart: Goat Rock's Buried History

Goat Rock Beach looks like a fixed point on the map -- a sea stack, a river mouth, a name on Highway 1. It is not fixed. The rock has been dynamited, the jetty it fed has been abandoned and sand-buried, and even the name "Goat Rock" has no confirmed origin. The beach's human history is mostly a record of people trying to change this stretch of coast, and the coast outlasting most of the attempts.

## Before There Was a Name For It

The sea stacks along this coast are Franciscan Complex rock, formed roughly 200 to 30 million years ago as the Farallon Plate collided with the North American Plate -- the same collision zone that put the active San Andreas Fault close to this shoreline today. Long after that, according to Wikipedia, mammoths are believed to have roamed the area as recently as 40,000 years ago, rubbing against the uplifted sea-stack rocks nearby and leaving smooth, abnormally worn patches behind. Retired state archaeologist Breck Parkman, in an interview with the Press Democrat, gives a narrower and more recent window for that same rubbing activity: 10,000 to 20,000 years ago. The two figures do not agree, and nothing in the sourced record resolves which is right -- only that both come from people who have looked closely at the same worn rock.

The earliest known human settlement of the site was by the Coast Miwok and Pomo tribes, with archaeological finds recorded as early as 1849. In 1905, the zoologist and ethnographer C. Hart Merriam visited Jenner and photographed a Pomo village, A-kow-wah-tol, situated near the river's mouth. Of that village, only artifact traces remain today. By the time the site passed into Mexican-era land administration, it fell within Rancho Bodega, one of the land grants that carved up this coastline long before "Goat Rock" was a name on any map.

## A Name With No Paper Trail

The name itself is a small mystery. "Goat Rock" does not appear on maps before World War I, which suggests it was coined sometime around that conflict -- but the popular explanation, that goatherds once grazed animals on or near the rock, has no documented evidence behind it in the sourced record. The name arrived from somewhere. Nobody currently has the paper trail to say from where.

## Blown Apart for a Jetty

The most consequential thing that ever happened to Goat Rock was not natural. On Nov. 1, 1929, the Healdsburg Tribune reported plans to blast the rock -- described then as 100 feet high -- apart to supply material for a jetty at the mouth of the Russian River in Jenner. Sixteen days later, on Nov. 17, 1929, crowds gathered to watch crews detonate 14 tons of black powder and dynamite, sending roughly 50,000 tons of crushed rock down for use in the jetty. A landmark now protected as a fragile harbor-seal rookery and marine conservation area was, less than a century ago, treated as a quarry.

Sonoma Water's environmental resource manager later confirmed that the jetty was built in phases between 1929 and 1948 -- nearly two decades of intermittent construction -- to keep a passage open for transporting mined gravel from the river, with fish passage cited as an early additional goal. The finished structure measured 1,600 feet long, 10 feet high and 40 feet wide, built entirely from rock quarried out of Goat Rock itself. It did not hold. In the winter of 1933-34, the Civil Works Administration paid 14 relief workers to repair an estimated $6,000 of storm damage to the jetty, work documented in a 1934 California Highway & Public Works article. Around the same period, in the 1930s, Civilian Conservation Corps Company 572 built a parking lot above Jenner Beach and trails down to the sand, part of the same New Deal-era push to make this stretch of coast usable.

The jetty eventually failed at its job well enough that it was abandoned and buried in sand. In 2014, a feasibility report required by the 2008 Russian River Biological Opinion studied whether the buried structure should be modified or removed to improve estuary habitat. The study's own author was blunt about its limits: the Biological Opinion required the study, not any funding to act on it, and no money was allocated for removal or modification. Nearly a century after the dynamite, the jetty still sits where it fell, unfunded and unresolved either way.

## An Afternoon in 1937, and a Movie Crew in 1985

Not everything documented here is about construction or catastrophe. On Nov. 5, 1937, the Oak Leaf, Santa Rosa Junior College's student newspaper, described a group of students spending a day at Goat Rock taking photographs, picnicking, and lighting a bonfire at dusk -- an ordinary outing, notable now mainly because someone wrote it down. Nearly fifty years later, in 1985, the beach found a very different kind of audience: viewed toward Arched Rock, it stood in for "Cauldron Point" in the final scene of the cult film The Goonies, the single fact about Goat Rock Beach most likely to be recognized by a visitor who has never set foot in Sonoma County.

## What the History Adds Up To

None of these threads -- the mammoths, the Pomo village, the dynamite, the failed jetty, the movie crew -- resolve into a single tidy story. What they share is a pattern: people have been altering, naming, quarrying, filming and photographing this rock for as long as there is a written record of it, and the rock and the river have mostly gone on doing what erosion, uplift and tides were always going to do regardless. The jetty blown out of Goat Rock in 1929 is still there, buried and unresolved, a physical reminder that the coast here does not stay fixed just because something got built on it.
