# The Saint on the Hill: Clifton Beach's Shrine, Storms, and a Bombing

Clifton Beach is built around a contradiction it never resolves. On the bluff above the sand sits the grave of a man Karachi credits with keeping the sea in its place. In 2010, men who considered that belief an offense against Islam killed at least seven people at his own door. Both things are documented. Neither cancels the other out.

## Who Is Buried on the Hill

Abdullah Shah Ghazi was a Muslim mystic, traditionally described as a descendant of the Prophet's family, who traveled to Sindh in the 8th century and died there around 773 CE. He was buried on a low sandy hill about 25 meters above the Arabian Sea, at what is now the western end of Clifton Beach. For most of its history the grave was unremarkable. Photographs from the late 1940s and early 1950s show little more than a shaky hut on a sandy hilltop. It grew into one of Karachi's largest Sufi shrines through two deliberate expansions: under the Ayub Khan government in the mid-1960s, and again under Zulfikar Ali Bhutto's government through the 1970s. It was neglected and shabby through the Zia-ul-Haq years of the 1980s, then rebuilt once more in a 2005–2007 renovation that coincided with the redevelopment of the waterfront park around it.

## The Belief That Cyclones Turn Away

Karachi sits on a stretch of the Arabian Sea capable of producing cyclones, and the World Beach Tour's own storm-track data records two tropical cyclones passing close enough to the beach to register in the last 50 years, peaking at Category 2 — a genuinely low count for a warm, cyclone-capable coast fronting a city of more than 20 million. Karachi has been forecast to take direct hits from storms that turned away late, including Cyclone Phet in 2010, Nilofar in 2014, Tauktae in 2021, and Biparjoy in 2023. Each near-miss gets attributed, in popular belief, to the saint on the hill.

The belief predates that recent run of storms. Journalist Salman Rashid documented a 1987 episode in which a rumored tsunami threat to Karachi circulated and was, in local telling, turned away by the same saint — "once again Abdullah Shah Ghazi had saved Karachi," as the story was recounted at the time — though no independent seismological or tidal record confirms a tsunami actually threatened the coast that year. Karachi's coastline was also, separately, untouched by the actual 26 December 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami, which killed roughly 230,000 people around the ocean's rim and devastated coasts from Aceh to Somalia; the documented reason lies in the Arabian Sea's shelf geometry and the angle of Pakistan's coast relative to the earthquake's rupture zone off Sumatra, not in a grave. Meteorologists and oceanographers attribute none of these outcomes to Abdullah Shah Ghazi. The belief persists regardless, because a city that has watched storms devastate other coastlines nearby wanted an explanation it could stand in front of, and the shrine was already there.

## The Bombing

On the evening of Thursday, 7 October 2010 — the shrine's busiest night of the week — two suicide bombers detonated within minutes of each other, one at the entrance and one inside the compound. At least seven people were killed and more than sixty wounded. No group claimed the attack by name, but it matched a documented pattern: militant groups in Pakistan had been attacking Sufi shrines across the country in this period, treating the veneration of saints as a corruption of Islamic monotheism worth destroying by force. A saint credited with protecting the city was attacked at his own grave by people who considered that protection itself a heresy.

The shrine reopened. Security screening at the entrance is now routine, a direct legacy of that night. By most visitor accounts in the years since, the underlying belief in the saint's protective power did not measurably weaken — if anything, the attack became folded into the same narrative of a sacred site under siege.

## What Grew Up Around the Grave

The shrine did not sit alone on its hill for long. In 1919 the Parsi philanthropist Jehangir Hormasji Kothari funded a stone promenade and pier nearby; the foundation stone was laid that February by Sir George Lloyd, Governor of Bombay, and the complex opened in stages through 1921 at a reported cost of about 300,000 British Indian rupees. In 1965 the Karachi Metropolitan Corporation opened Funland immediately beside the shrine — an amusement park with rides that included dodgem cars, drawing crowds for four decades before losing its aquarium (Machli Ghar), bowling alley, and most of its footprint when the city built the 130-acre Bagh Ibn-i-Qasim park around the old Kothari Parade between 2005 and 2007. The devotional and the recreational have shared this hill for a century, largely without acknowledging each other.

## Reading the Hill Today

Visitors can walk up to the shrine's courtyard, which remains open to people of all faiths, after passing through security screening put in place since 2010. From the top, the view runs back down over the crowded sand of Sea View, the camel and horse handlers working the shoreline, and the haze of a city of more than 20 million people that has decided, against the documented record, that the grave on this hill is what has kept the water out. Whatever else Clifton Beach is — polluted in places, underprotected for swimmers, argued over by regulators — it is also the site of that specific, ongoing argument between folklore and force.
