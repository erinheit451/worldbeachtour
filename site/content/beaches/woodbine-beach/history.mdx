# Twenty More Years: Who Actually Runs Woodbine Beach

Woodbine is the largest beach in Toronto, a 15.2-hectare park created in 1950 on a stretch of shoreline that, before Lake Shore Boulevard was extended to Woodbine Avenue in the 1950s, was a wooded area locals called "the cut," not a bathing beach at all. That history matters, because everything that has happened to Woodbine since has been a decision the city made, not a fact of nature. Two of those decisions, one about food and drink, one about who gets to fund a plan, define the beach's politics more than its sand does.

## One Family, One Contract, Two Decades Renewed

The only place on Woodbine or the eastern beaches licensed to sell food and alcohol has been controlled by George Foulidis and his family for more than 20 years. In 2007, city council voted to renew that concession for 20 more years, and it did so without opening the contract to competitive bidding. The vote was pushed through by then Ward 32 councillor Sandra Bussin, whose ward includes the beach.

The Toronto Star laid out the arrangement in a July 2010 investigation, framed as a look at what was wrong with the city's "crown jewel" waterfront. The paper's on-the-ground reporting found a single restaurant at the beach at the time, the Boardwalk Pub, in the parking lot on Lake Shore Blvd. East, charging $11 for gourmet burgers and fries, tourist-attraction prices at a public beach with no competing option. No source in the research for this page confirms whether the Foulidis concession or the Boardwalk Pub is still the operating arrangement today; the sourced facts here are pinned to 2010 and should be read as a description of that arrangement's origin and renewal, not its current state.

Bussin herself, defending the beach's status quo generally, recalled a predecessor's warning: "Don't entertain too much change on the beach. People won't accept it." It is a telling line to attach to a story about a no-bid contract renewal. The warning was framed as protecting the beach's character. In practice, it also protected an arrangement that had never faced a competitor.

## The Courts Tell the Same Story

Woodbine's other headline feature, sand volleyball, runs on a similar structure. The beach has roughly 95 courts in the sand, more than anywhere else in the city, but only 10 of them are open to the public for free. The other 85 are operated by the Ontario Volleyball Association, and playing on them requires an adult league membership starting at $45 for the summer. A beach marketed as the city's biggest public playground has, in practice, handed most of its signature activity to a private membership organization.

Neither the concession nor the courts arrangement is illegal, and neither shows up as a scandal in the ordinary sense: nobody was charged with anything, and the deals were made in public council votes and public league structures. What they share is a pattern. At Toronto's most heavily used public beach, the city has repeatedly chosen to hand the parts people actually pay for, food, drink, organized volleyball, to a single family or a single private association, rather than run them itself or open them to competition.

## A Master Plan That Never Got Built

The same pattern shows up in what the city didn't do. In 2008, Waterfront Toronto produced a master plan, Lake Ontario Park, envisioning more than 37 kilometres of connected shoreline park from Cherry Beach to the eastern beaches, including a promenade linking Ashbridge's Bay to Tommy Thompson Park estimated at roughly $17 million. No source available for this page confirms the promenade, or the wider master plan, was ever funded or built.

That same year offered a smaller, more concrete measure of the same problem. A section of the Ashbridge's Bay boardwalk, right next to Woodbine, blew out in a storm. It took the city more than a year to repair it, for lack of funding. A beach with a $17-million vision on paper couldn't find the money to fix a broken boardwalk on a normal repair timeline.

## What This Adds Up To

None of this makes Woodbine a badly run beach in the operational sense. It is Blue Flag certified, has been since 2005; the water is tested daily; lifeguards work the sand every summer; and the city has machine-groomed the sand daily since a real turnaround in water and sand quality that it dates to 1990. Toronto's largest beach is, in the ordinary maintenance sense, a well-kept one.

The politics sitting underneath that maintenance are a different question. A single family has run the only food-and-alcohol concession for more than two decades, renewed without a competitive bid. A private association controls 85 of roughly 95 volleyball courts. A funded, ambitious public plan for the whole waterfront stalled with no confirmed outcome, while a storm-damaged boardwalk sat broken for over a year. Toronto's biggest, busiest, most public beach keeps ending up run, in the parts that cost money to use, by somebody other than the public.
