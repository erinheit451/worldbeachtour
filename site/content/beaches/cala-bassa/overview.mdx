Cala Bassa is a north-facing cove on Ibiza's northwest coast, in the municipality of Sant Josep de sa Talaia. Measured straight-line from this page's own coordinates, it sits about 17.9 kilometers northwest of Ibiza Town. Wikipedia's prose figure of 24.9 kilometers (15.5 mi) and the Sant Josep council's tourism-site figure of 21 km are both larger than that — neither source states its method, but both land in road-trip range rather than straight-line range, so they're best read as road-scale estimates rather than a second straight-line measurement. By road, the practical base is Sant Antoni de Portmany, 10.9 km (about 19 minutes) away; Ibiza Airport is 24.5 km (about 41 minutes) by road, though the beach dataset's own figures for both (5.7 km and 15.6 km) are straight-line distances, not driving distances.

The cove faces north and is closed by limestone headlands on both sides, sabina junipers and pines on the slope above the sand. A sediment model (GloPrSM) classifies the beach as 59.1% quartz, 3.5% feldspar, 37.4% lithic grains — a mixed composition consistent with Ibiza's limestone, dolomite and marl bedrock rather than a coral or pure-quartz beach. The EU's bathing water register rates the water "2 - good" at monitoring station ES531M0481210, one tier below "excellent."

## What to Expect

The beach has restrooms, a wheelchair-accessible ramp to the sand, and parking, but no showers, according to the Sant Josep council's own tourism listing. A lifeguard operates in summer season only; outside July–August, or off-season, there is no rescue cover. Cala Bassa Beach Club, run by the CBbC Group, is the one beach-facing business with a verifiable operating history — multiple dining areas, sunbeds, and a "beach boy" service that brings food and drink to the sand. Sant Josep's tourism office also lists unnamed "restaurants and chiringuitos" along the shoreline, without giving specifics that could be verified independently.

What is not visible from the sand is what makes this cove a subject of active government enforcement: a Posidonia oceanica seagrass meadow on the seabed, protected under a 2018 Balearic decree, and named by regional patrols as part of a "problem area" for illegal anchoring in 2025. Nine boats were cited in a single sweep across Sant Antoni Bay and Cala Bassa that August; the reporting doesn't break down how many were at Cala Bassa itself. The full account, including the enforcement numbers and what's being done about them, is in the [environment deep-dive](/beaches/cala-bassa/environment).

## Most Relevant Lenses

**Travel** — Cala Bassa has no direct public bus connection outside the summer season and no accommodation of its own; visitors base themselves in Sant Antoni de Portmany or arrive by rental car, seasonal bus, or the Cruceros Portmany boat that runs from Sant Antoni harbor. The full logistics are in the [travel guide](/beaches/cala-bassa/travel).

**Environment** — The Posidonia meadow beneath the cove, the 2018 anchoring ban that protects it, and the Balearic government's own 2025 enforcement data naming this stretch of coast as a problem area are the specific, documented reason this beach is more than a postcard cove. Covered in full in the environment deep-dive.

**Snorkeling** — The rocky western margin carries a municipal snorkel route, rated "advanced" by the Sant Josep tourism office, that passes a partly submerged sea cave known locally as the Cueva de los Sastres (Tailors' Cave). The office's own guidance recommends a marker buoy on that route given the boat traffic overhead.

**Family** — The central strip of sand has a sandy bottom and a gentle slope into the water, which is the section Sant Josep's tourism office itself describes as suited to families; the wheelchair ramp extends that access to visitors who need it.

**Photography** — Because the cove faces north, Cala Bassa does not get a classic sunset over open water; late-afternoon light instead lands on the limestone slope and pine cover above the sand, a different subject than the water itself.

## Practical Context

Cala Bassa reads, from the sand, like an unremarkable calm cove with a beach club on it. The government paperwork underneath it — a seagrass meadow, a decree with fines up to €450,000, and a named 2025 enforcement action — is the part that doesn't show up in a photograph, and it's the more accurate description of what this specific stretch of Ibiza coastline is right now.
