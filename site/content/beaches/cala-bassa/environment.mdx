# What's Under Cala Bassa's Water

Cala Bassa's water is clear enough to see the seabed from the surface across most of the cove, and what that clarity reveals is a meadow of Posidonia oceanica, a flowering marine plant endemic to the Mediterranean. It is not decoration. It is the reason the water is clear in the first place — Posidonia meadows trap sediment and slow currents, the opposite of what a torn-up, anchor-scarred seabed does — and it is also the specific thing a Balearic government patrol boat is looking for when it checks a mooring in this bay.

## A Plant, Not Seaweed

Posidonia oceanica is a seagrass: a flowering plant that lives entirely underwater, distinct from algae or seaweed, and found only in the Mediterranean basin. It spreads by growing rhizomes — root-like horizontal stems — across the seabed at a rate of roughly 1 to 6 centimeters a year. A meadow that looks unremarkable from a paddleboard can be centuries old. In the strait between Ibiza and Formentera, south of Cala Bassa, researchers identified in 2006 a single clonal colony of Posidonia estimated at up to 100,000 years old, among the oldest living organisms yet documented anywhere. That specific colony is not in Cala Bassa's own bay — it sits in the Ses Salines strait, the stretch of coast that earned Ibiza its 1999 UNESCO "Biodiversity and Culture" listing — but it is the same species, growing under the same law, that carpets the seabed here.

The ecological case for protecting it is not sentimental. A hectare of established Posidonia absorbs roughly 15 times more carbon dioxide annually than an equivalent hectare of Amazon rainforest, according to researchers who study the species' carbon sequestration. The meadow also stabilizes the sand it grows near, which is one reason beaches fringed by healthy Posidonia tend to lose less sand to erosion than beaches without it.

## The Law That Protects It

On 27 July 2018 the Balearic government published Decreto 25/2018 in the Butlletí Oficial de les Illes Balears, banning the anchoring of vessels on Posidonia oceanica as a general rule across the entire archipelago — not just inside marine reserves. The fines are tiered by severity: €100 to €6,000 for a minor (leve) infraction, up to €100,000 for a serious (grave) one, and up to €450,000 if a boat is found to have destroyed a meadow outright (muy grave). Before the decree, prosecuting an anchoring violation generally required proving actual ecological damage after the fact. The 2018 rule removed that burden: dropping an anchor on the seagrass is itself the offense, whether or not the plant survives the encounter.

## The Numbers at This Beach

The law's existence hasn't closed the gap between rule and behavior. The Balearic government's Posidonia Surveillance Service — patrol boats funded partly through the EU's Next Generation recovery plan — closed its 2025 season having logged 181,468 mooring inspections and interventions across the four islands, a 40% increase over the 129,497 recorded in 2024. Broken out by island, Ibiza's patrols found 21% of the boats they checked moored directly on seagrass, compared with 7% in Mallorca, 5% in Menorca, and just 2% in Formentera. The government's own season summary named Sant Antoni Bay — the stretch of coast that includes Cala Bassa — as one of a handful of islandwide "problem areas."

That designation turned into a specific enforcement action on 13 August 2025, when regional environmental agents cited nine separate vessels for anchoring on Posidonia in Sant Antoni Bay and at Cala Bassa in a single sweep. Officials told Diario de Ibiza that nautical traffic in the area had grown noticeably in recent years — a plausible explanation for a cove with a beach club, a public boat service, and clear, calm water that makes an obvious place to anchor.

## What's Supposed to Fix It

The regulatory answer to "stop anchoring on the meadow" is "give boats somewhere else to go." In March 2026, Spain's Ministry for Ecological Transition and the Digital Challenge approved a set of ecological mooring-buoy zones along Ibiza's coastline — fixed lines a boat can clip onto instead of dropping an anchor — naming seven sites: Benirràs, Cala Salada, Portinatx, Talamanca, Cala Vedella, es Xarco, and Cala Bassa itself. The concept isn't new or untested elsewhere in the Mediterranean, but rollout on Ibiza has been uneven: Diario de Ibiza reported in May 2026 that Talamanca and six other Ibiza beaches — Cala Bassa among them — still lacked their planned buoy fields heading into that summer's peak season. Cala Bassa's own anchorage is on the approved list, but as of that May 2026 report, still without its buoy field.

## For a Snorkeler, Specifically

None of this makes Cala Bassa unsafe or unpleasant to swim in — the EU rates its bathing water "2 - good," and the meadow itself is part of what makes the water worth swimming in. On the cove's rocky western margin, Sant Josep's municipal tourism office maps an official snorkel route, rated "advanced," that runs past rocky outcrops partially covered in Posidonia and a partly submerged sea cave known locally as the Cueva de los Sastres (Tailors' Cave), which opens from underwater into a fully air-filled vault. The office's own guidance recommends carrying a marker buoy on that route, given how much boat traffic passes overhead — the same traffic this page is otherwise about.

The honest way to think about the seagrass under Cala Bassa is as a resource with a paper trail: a growth rate slow enough to measure in centimeters per year, a law with real fines behind it, and a 2025 government report that put this beach's own bay on a named list of places where the law and the water don't yet match.
