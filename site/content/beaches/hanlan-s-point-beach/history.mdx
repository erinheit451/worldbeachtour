# The 1894 By-law: How Toronto Accidentally Built a Landmark

Hanlan's Point Beach is historically significant because of a single piece of Victorian municipal law, not because of any feature of the shoreline itself. On 17 July 1894, Toronto City Council passed By-law No. 3268, designating a 200-by-50-foot strip of this beach where bathing without a suit was legally permitted. City archives hold the original text as By-law No. 3268, vol. 1 (1894), App. B. The International Naturist Federation, writing on the designation in 2025, calls it the world's oldest official legal nude-beach zone — a claim that rests on one advocacy organization's research rather than an exhaustive global survey, but one no older documented municipal nude-bathing law has been found to contradict.

## Containment, Not Tolerance

Nineteenth-century Toronto had no shortage of complaints about undressed swimmers along its waterfront. Council's solution in 1894 was not to ban the practice outright but to zone it: confine it to one small, defined patch of sand at the point, away from the more genteel excursion crowds using the rest of the island. That distinction matters for how the beach's later reputation should be read — the city was managing a nuisance complaint, not endorsing a freedom. The permission did not survive unchallenged. A 1930 by-law eliminated the clothing-optional zone entirely, and it would stay eliminated for almost seventy years.

## What Replaced It: Baseball, Fires, and an Airport

For most of the interwar period, Hanlan's Point's public identity had nothing to do with nudity. Hanlan's Point Stadium, built in 1897 and rebuilt after fires in 1903 and 1909, was home to the minor-league Toronto Maple Leafs from 1897–1901 and 1908–1925. On 5 September 1914, a 19-year-old Providence Grays pitcher named Babe Ruth threw a one-hitter here and hit his first professional home run in a 9-0 win — the single most famous individual event this point has ever hosted, and one that has nothing to do with the beach's other history.

The stadium and its amusement park were demolished in 1937 to build what opened in 1939 as Port George VI Island Airport, now Billy Bishop Toronto City Airport. 37 cottages were removed for the airport, and 31 of them were moved by barge to Algonquin Island (then Sunfish Island) in 1938. For roughly two decades, the point that had briefly been a legal nude beach was baseball and amusement-park infrastructure; for the decades after 1937, it has been the edge of an active airport runway.

## The Queer History That Outlasted the Law

What survived the 1930 repeal was informal, not legal. By the 1940s, gay and lesbian Torontonians had begun using the same stretch of shoreline as an unofficial gathering place — a pattern Toronto activist George Hislop was already describing as an established fact by 1958, more than a decade before it became a public, organized event. On 1 August 1971, the Community Homophile Association of Toronto and allied groups held the Gay Day picnic at Hanlan's Point, generally counted as Canada's first Pride gathering.

The city's tolerance of that use was thin. In August 1975, Toronto police raided the beach and charged 62 people — 59 of them men — with indecent exposure, as The Globe and Mail reported that month, a case that moved through the courts into 1976 alongside further arrests and confrontations, including one involving Dennis Colby. The community kept returning anyway. The city did not restore legal clothing-optional status until a one-year pilot project in 1999, extended in 2000 and made permanent in 2002.

## 2023: The City Catches Up

In 2023, Toronto City Council expanded the clothing-optional zone from a defined section to the entire beach and formally recognized Hanlan's Point as one of the ten oldest surviving queer spaces anywhere in the world — a single designation that retroactively links the 1894 by-law, the 1971 picnic, and the 1975 raid into one continuous claim on the site, rather than three disconnected episodes separated by decades of legal reversal. Council also committed to a Pride flagpole and ecological restoration of the site.

That commitment landed at an awkward moment. By 2026, erosion had stripped much of Hanlan's Point down to a fraction of its historic sand, and the restoration plan promised alongside the heritage designation had not broken ground. Reporting from CityNews Toronto and CTV News Toronto that year described advocates pushing the city over the slow pace of its response — the same institutional lag, in different form, that separated the 1930 repeal from the 1999 pilot and the 1975 raid from the 2023 recognition. The city has consistently arrived late to formalizing what people were already doing on this sand.

## Why the Bylaw, Not the Beach, Is the Story

Hanlan's Point is not exceptional as a stretch of Lake Ontario shoreline: the wave record here shows a calm annual mean height of about half a meter, no different in kind from other sheltered lake beaches nearby, and 2026 erosion has made the physical beach smaller than it has been in decades. What makes it exceptional is the paper trail — a bylaw drafted to solve a Victorian nuisance complaint that happened to create the physical space where, unplanned, one of the world's oldest continuously used queer gathering sites took root. The 1894 council members who wrote By-law No. 3268 were not building a landmark. The landmark built itself, out of whoever kept returning to that 200-by-50-foot strip of sand through baseball, fire, demolition, arrest, and repeal.
