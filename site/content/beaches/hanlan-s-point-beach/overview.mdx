Hanlan's Point Beach occupies the western tip of the Toronto Islands, a chain of islands and sandbars in Lake Ontario reached by a roughly 15-minute ferry crossing from the Jack Layton Ferry Terminal at the foot of Bay Street. The beach and the point are named for Ned Hanlan, world sculling champion from 1880 to 1884, whose father built a small hotel on this shoreline in the 1860s. What makes the beach notable is not its size or scenery but a single piece of 19th-century municipal law: on 17 July 1894, Toronto City Council passed By-law No. 3268, legalizing nude bathing on a 200-by-50-foot strip of this sand, a designation the International Naturist Federation calls the world's oldest official nude-beach zoning.

## What's Actually Here

The beach today is split in practice, not by any visible barrier: a southern, clothing-optional section — expanded to cover the entire beach by city council in 2023 — and a northern, textile section used by families and casual swimmers. Both share the same shoreline, which by 2026 had lost much of its sand to erosion the city had pledged, but not yet begun, to address. Billy Bishop Toronto City Airport sits 1.4 km away in a straight line at the point's northwest edge; arriving and departing aircraft are visible and audible from the sand, but there is no public path connecting the airport grounds to the beach. Nothing on the airport side is walkable — reaching it means going back through the ferry system to the mainland.

## The History Under the Sand

The site's identity has changed more completely, more often, than most urban beaches. Between 1897 and 1925, Hanlan's Point Stadium stood here, home to the minor-league Toronto Maple Leafs; on 5 September 1914, a 19-year-old Providence Grays pitcher named Babe Ruth hit his first professional home run in a game played on this point. The stadium and an amusement park were demolished in 1937 to build what became Billy Bishop Toronto City Airport; 37 cottages were removed for the airport, and 31 of them were moved by barge to Algonquin Island (then Sunfish Island) in 1938. Meanwhile, the nude-bathing designation that had been repealed in 1930 gave way to something the by-law never anticipated: by the 1940s, the same beach had become an established gathering place for gay and lesbian Torontonians, culminating in the Gay Day picnic of 1 August 1971 — generally counted as Canada's first Pride event — and a police raid in August 1975, reported that month by The Globe and Mail, that charged 62 people with indecent exposure. Clothing-optional status returned through a 1999 pilot and was made permanent in 2002; in 2023, the city formally recognized Hanlan's Point as one of the ten oldest surviving queer spaces in the world.

## Most Relevant Lens

**History** is where this beach's real content lives — the 1894 by-law, the baseball stadium turned airport, the 1971 picnic, and the 1975 raid form a continuous, documented throughline that has little to do with the physical qualities of the sand itself. **Travel** planning matters more here than at most beaches, since access is ferry-only, there are no vendors on-site, and the two social zones (clothing-optional and textile) require knowing which end of the beach you're heading toward before you arrive.

## Practical Context

Hanlan's Point rewards visitors who come for what happened here rather than for postcard scenery: the wave record shows a calm annual mean height of about half a meter, unremarkable for a sheltered lake beach, and 2026 erosion has left the shoreline visibly narrower than in past decades. What sets it apart is documentation — a 132-year-old by-law, a Babe Ruth home run, a Pride origin story, and a police raid, all attached to the same 200-by-50-foot patch of sand.
