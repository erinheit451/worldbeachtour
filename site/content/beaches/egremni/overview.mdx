Egremni is a pebble beach at the base of a limestone cliff on the south-western coast of Lefkada, an Ionian island in north-western Greece, near the village of Athani. It has one of the most complicated access histories of any Greek beach: a staircase destroyed by an earthquake, rebuilt over six years, and now closed again by municipal order since 2025.

## A Cove Made Famous by a Road, Then Unmade by an Earthquake

A sealed road built in the mid-1990s turned Egremni from a remote spot into one of Lefkada's premier tourist beaches, reached by a mountain staircase of 347 steep steps. That changed on 17 November 2015, when a 6.5-magnitude earthquake struck Lefkada. A flank of the cliff collapsed onto the beach, burying the sunbathing terrace and destroying the staircase; because it happened in November, no one was on the sand. Elsewhere on the island, the quake killed two elderly women — a 69-year-old struck by a boulder that hit her house, and an 82-year-old killed by a collapsing wall.

Rebuilding took nearly six years and outlasted a pandemic. The road and a new staircase, roughly 420 steps, were reported complete by 12 May 2021 and reopened for that summer, after five straight closed summers.

## Closed Again Since 2025

Following a fresh geological risk assessment, the Municipality of Lefkada formally closed the road and staircase again starting in 2025, citing severe ground instability — a closure that has not been lifted, as reported by the Vasiliki tourism portal vasiliki.eu. Storms in late February 2026 (reported 25 February) then damaged the staircase with rockfall; it had reportedly gone about four years without a municipal inspection. By 1 July 2026, a private, non-municipal initiative, not the municipality, had cleared the debris and repaired the railings, but no city engineer had inspected or certified that work. Boat operators out of Vasiliki and Nydri keep advertising and docking at Egremni regardless.

## Getting a Sense of the Place

Egremni pairs naturally with its neighbor Porto Katsiki, about 4 km away along the coast (further by road) — both are cliff-bound white-pebble coves on Lefkada's western edge, and boat operators often combine them in one trip. There is no lifeguard service at Egremni, and entry is steep pebble with a quick offshore drop-off.

**History** — The full staircase-to-closure story, earthquake to present, is its own deep dive: see the linked history page below.

**Access** — With the land route closed, Vasiliki's daily cruise (€40 adult, €20 child, up to 200 passengers) or an eight-hour Nydri cruise (roughly $46-51) are the ways in that local tourism sites recommend.

**Honesty about the risk** — Egremni is not dangerous by accident. It is a beach the local government has told people to stay off, reachable via a staircase a private, non-municipal initiative, not an engineer, decided was safe enough to use again. That tension, not the scenery, is the story worth knowing before you go.
