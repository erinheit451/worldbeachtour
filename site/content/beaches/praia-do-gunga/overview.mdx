Praia do Gunga is a sand spit in Roteiro, a municipality of roughly 6,500 people on Alagoas' southern coast, separating the open Atlantic from the Lagoa do Roteiro — the tidal lagoon formed where the Rio São Miguel meets the sea. The beach is not part of Barra de São Miguel, the town most visitors actually stage from; it belongs to a separate municipality, even though nearly every boat that reaches it departs from Barra's marina across the water. English Wikipedia's own entry files the beach under "Neighbourhoods in Maceió," which is incorrect — Gunga sits roughly 43 km south of the state capital by road, in Roteiro.

## What's Actually There

The postcard image — coconut palms along a sand spit, water shifting from blue to green with the tide — is real. So is the working tourist economy underneath it. By 2017, complex administrator Edinaldo Pulcino reported the beach supported 14 bar-restaurants, 18 food stalls and 32 craft stalls, drawing an estimated 5,000 visitors a month at peak and supporting roughly 600 direct jobs. That same year, Alagoas' state environmental institute (IMA/AL) and the Roteiro municipal government began negotiating a Termo de Ajustamento de Conduta — the legal mechanism Brazil uses to bring an already-built commercial operation into compliance after the fact. One of the images most reused to sell Alagoas tourism sat on a peninsula whose permitting was still catching up to its economy.

The ecology has its own presence. In January 2018, a broad-snouted caiman over two meters long turned up on the sand — a species locally extinct in Alagoas for roughly two decades before a breeding facility in Maceió's Fernão Velho neighborhood helped reestablish it. The ocean-facing side carries real surf: an annual mean wave height of 1.46 m, peaking near 1.8 m in June, and a rip current the beach's own wave dataset flags directly. The lagoon-facing side is calm by geography, not by any managed intervention, and it's the side worth choosing for children or weak swimmers.

## The Honest Version

There are no lifeguards here. When a speedboat lost power and hit rocks nearby on 11 September 2017, injuring six people, it was jangadeiros — traditional raft fishermen — who pulled the victims out, not any official rescue service. On 1 December 2018, a 60-year-old man drowned at the beach; emergency crews could not revive him. None of this makes Gunga unusual among Brazilian ocean beaches without lifeguard service — it makes it typical of one, marketed with none of that context attached.

## Getting Oriented

Two sides, one spit: the Atlantic side for real surf and photography, the Lagoa do Roteiro side for calmer, shallower water. In between sits the beach complex — bar-restaurants, food stalls, craft stalls — that, as of the April 2025 TAC, faced a fresh 30-day compliance-filing deadline and still had no recorded operating licence; whether the state has issued one since isn't documented here. Access is by boat from Barra de São Miguel's marina or by a road that crosses private land; there is no public through-road. What Gunga rewards is treating it as what it is: a genuinely striking piece of coastline running on an informal, functioning local economy, not a resort with the infrastructure to match its own marketing.
