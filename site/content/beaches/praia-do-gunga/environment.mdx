# The Peninsula That Outran Its Paperwork

Praia do Gunga is a sand spit in Roteiro, Alagoas, separating the open Atlantic from the Lagoa do Roteiro, the tidal lagoon where the Rio São Miguel meets the sea. The geography is what makes the beach photogenic — the water reads bluer at low tide and greener at high tide as the two systems trade places. The geography is also what makes the beach's environmental story specific: a narrow, ecologically active landform carrying a large, dense tourist operation, with the regulatory paperwork for that operation still catching up years after it was built.

## A Complex Bigger Than Its Footprint

By July 2017, according to complex administrator Edinaldo Pulcino, the beach supported 14 bar-restaurants, 18 food stalls and 32 craft stalls — 64 individual commercial operations on one sand spit, in a municipality of roughly 6,500 people. Pulcino estimated monthly visitor traffic at 5,000 in high season and 2,000 otherwise, and put employment at roughly 600 direct jobs and 3,000 indirect ones.

That density on a narrow spit of sand between two water bodies is precisely why environmental regulators got involved. On 7 July 2017, Gustavo Lopes, director-president of Alagoas' state environmental institute (IMA/AL), met with Roteiro mayor Wladimir Brito and Pulcino to open talks on a Termo de Ajustamento de Conduta (TAC) — Brazil's standard legal mechanism for bringing an already-built commercial operation into compliance with environmental law retroactively. The meeting was described as the first of several planned sessions. TACs exist because so much of Brazil's coastline develops first and gets licensed later, if at all; Gunga's complex, by its own administrator's numbers, is a fully developed example of that pattern.

## Access Runs Through Someone's Land

The environmental informality has a territorial twin. There is no public road connecting Praia do Gunga to the highway network. English-language reference sources describe the land route simply as arriving "by car driving through a private property." Visitors without a vehicle instead cross the Lagoa do Roteiro by boat from Barra de São Miguel's marina, a trip of roughly fifteen to twenty minutes.

Road-routing calculations make the indirectness concrete: a drive from Zumbi dos Palmares International Airport (MCZ) to the beach runs about 64 km and 68 minutes by the shortest routable path, well above the 40.7 km straight-line distance between the two points. The gap exists because the coast road bends inland around the lagoon system before a final approach that isn't public right-of-way. A beach reused constantly in Alagoas tourism marketing is, physically, reached by favor rather than by public infrastructure.

## Wildlife That Moves Through, Not Around

The lagoon-and-ocean geography that draws tourists also functions as a live ecological corridor. In January 2018, beachgoers found a Caiman latirostris — a broad-snouted caiman over two meters long — on the sand. The Environmental Police Battalion (BPA) rescued the animal. The species had been locally extinct in Alagoas for roughly two decades before a captive-breeding facility in Maceió's Fernão Velho neighborhood helped reestablish a wild population; veterinarian Isaac Albuquerque explained that rising water in inland lagoons during the breeding season (November–March) periodically pushes individuals toward the coast. The caimans that occasionally turn up at Gunga are descendants of that reintroduction effort, not a natural holdout population that never left.

Eleven months later, in December 2018, a female melon-headed whale over two meters long stranded in shallow water and died despite a response from the Instituto Biota, which planned a necropsy to determine cause. Neither event is unique to Gunga among Alagoas beaches, but both are documented, dated instances of the same underlying fact: this peninsula sits inside an active marine and estuarine ecosystem, not a static backdrop for photographs.

## The Water Itself

<WeatherWidget />

The dataset behind this page describes Gunga's wave regime in blunt terms: "Exposed — real surf, and rip current to respect." Annual mean wave height runs 1.46 m, rising toward a June peak of 1.8 m (with a recorded peak-day figure of 2.2 m) and easing to about 1.18 m in February, per Open-Meteo's ERA5 marine reanalysis for 2023–2024. That's the ocean-facing side. The lagoon side, sheltered by the spit itself, is calm by geography rather than by any managed intervention — no breakwater, no dredging program on record, just the physical shape of the peninsula doing the work.

None of this water is staffed. There are no lifeguards on either side of the peninsula. When a speedboat lost power and struck rocks near the beach on 11 September 2017, injuring six people including two children, it was jangadeiros — independent traditional raft fishermen working nearby — who pulled the victims out, not any official rescue service. On 1 December 2018, a 60-year-old man drowned here; Fire Department, SAMU ambulance and the Falcão 5 aerial unit responded but could not revive him, and the cause was never established in available reporting.

## What the Environmental Picture Actually Shows

Put together, Gunga's environmental story isn't pollution or habitat loss in the usual sense — it's a permitting gap. A dense, economically significant tourist complex grew on a narrow, ecologically live peninsula faster than the state's compliance process could formalize it, while the physical access to that peninsula ran, and apparently still runs, through land the public has no guaranteed right to cross. The wildlife that occasionally surfaces on the sand — a recovering caiman population, a stranded whale — and the wave data that flags a real rip current are both reminders that the ecosystem underneath the postcard keeps operating on its own schedule, regardless of whether the paperwork above it has caught up.
