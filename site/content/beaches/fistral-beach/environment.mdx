# What's Actually in the Water at Fistral

Fistral Beach sits on the north Cornwall coast, half a mile west of Newquay, facing west-northwest into open Atlantic groundswell. The sand is modeled as feldspar-rich (quartz 31.7%, feldspar 45.3%, lithics 23.0%, per the GloPrSM v1.0.0 sediment model), consistent with Cornwall's granite geology, the same batholith that feeds the region's china-clay industry inland. Average significant wave height runs 2.06 meters a year, biggest in December at a 2.96-meter monthly average, calmest in June at 1.2 meters, with a peak recorded day of 5.26 meters (Open-Meteo Marine ERA5 reanalysis, 2023-2024). None of that geology or wave data is in dispute. What is contested, and increasingly litigated, is what else is in the water.

## The Discharge Numbers

South West Water operates the sewer network serving Newquay, including a series of combined sewer overflows (CSOs) designed to release excess wastewater into the sea during heavy rain rather than back up into homes. In 2024, South West Water recorded 221 sewage discharge incidents in the Newquay area, totaling 376 hours, an average of just over four discharges every week across the year. Surfers Against Sewage, the environmental campaign group that has tracked UK water quality since 1990, separately logged 162.8 hours of discharge specifically attributed to Fistral in 2025 and ranked it sixth worst among the ten UK surf spots it monitors most closely, writing that a beach this prominent placing so high on the list was "a travesty."

The discharges are not evenly distributed through the year; they cluster around heavy rainfall, when the sewer network's capacity is exceeded. Burst pipes and pumping-station failures near the Esplanade have separately caused spills that turned the water visibly brown, incidents distinct from the routine storm-overflow releases. The practical result for visitors is that water quality at Fistral is not fixed: an annual charity surf event was cancelled the day before it was scheduled to run in late November 2024 because of a discharge warning, and South West Water and Surfers Against Sewage both publish live alerts that change hour to hour.

## Why a Lawsuit Became Legally Possible

For decades, English nuisance law effectively shielded water companies from private claims over statutory sewage discharges, on the reasoning that a permitted discharge regime displaced the common-law right to sue. That changed in July 2024, when the UK Supreme Court ruled in Manchester Ship Canal Company Ltd v United Utilities Water Ltd that a private nuisance claim could proceed against a water company despite an existing statutory discharge framework. The case itself concerned an industrial canal in northwest England, not a beach, but the ruling reopened nuisance law for anyone affected by permitted discharges nationwide.

Law firm Leigh Day, which had already built a group claim against South West Water centered on Exmouth — following wild swimmer Jo Bateman's own action, and enrolling more than 1,400 claimants there — used the ruling to expand its case. On 6 February 2026 the firm issued a 58-page Letter Before Action covering Dawlish, Sidmouth, Teignmouth, Newquay, and Penzance, with roughly 1,900 claimants enrolled overall; the Newquay arm will initially address sewage incidents at Fistral Beach specifically before widening across the rest of Newquay. The letter invites South West Water to admit liability and propose settlement terms; South West Water has three months to respond, until 6 May 2026, or the firm says it will issue court proceedings.

Justine Taylor of the local advocacy group Clean Sea Newquay summarized the complaint: "Sewage in the sea ruins living here for everybody. Instead of working to protect the health of the public and the environment, South West Water has prioritised turning pollution into profit." Local surfer Rob Abrams put it in more immediate terms: kids who waited all year having their surf trip ruined, wild swimmers no longer wanting to get in, dog-walkers whose pets keep getting sick, and anxious beachside business owners. Neither statement claims South West Water has been found liable in court. Both describe why nearly 1,900 people signed on before any verdict.

## The Hazard That Predates the Sewage Story

Sewage is not the only thing that puts people at risk in Fistral's water, and it is worth separating the two. RNLI lifeguards patrol Fistral seasonally, June through September, and rip currents remain the beach's most immediate physical hazard, unrelated to water quality. On Saturday 26 July 2025, a powerful flash rip current formed outside the flagged swimming area and RNLI lifeguards rescued 17 people within a short window; similar multi-person rescues have recurred in past summers, including 16 to 19 people pulled from a single rip in July 2021. Swimming only between the red-and-yellow flags during lifeguard hours addresses the rip-current risk; it does not address water quality, which requires checking a separate live alert.

## What the Geology and the Sewage Story Have in Common

Cornwall's granite geology gave Fistral its feldspar-rich sand and, indirectly, its surf: the exposed headlands of Towan Head and Pentire Point East that frame the bay are part of the same resistant rock that shapes the north Cornwall coastline generally, funneling Atlantic groundswell into a bay that breaks reliably enough to have built an entire national surf culture around it since 1962. The sewage problem is a separate, newer layer: a Victorian-era-descended sewer network built for a much smaller town, now serving a resort population that swells sharply each summer, discharging through overflow valves that were designed as an emergency measure and now fire routinely. The beach's natural conditions are not implicated in its pollution problem, and its pollution problem does not change what the waves are doing. They are two different systems, sitting on top of each other, and as of 2026 one of them is now in front of a court.
