# The Beach Al-Shabab Keeps Bombing and Mogadishu Keeps Reclaiming

Lido Beach has been attacked by Al-Shabab at least five times since January 2016 — more than any other single site in Mogadishu — and it has reopened after every one of them. That pattern is not incidental. The group that now bombs Lido once banned people from gathering there at all, for exactly the same reason: it is Mogadishu's most visible symbol of secular, mixed-gender public leisure, and both the ban and the bombs are attempts to shut that symbol down.

## A Colonial Beach, a National One

The name comes from Italian: "lido" simply means "beach," and Italian colonial administrators developed this stretch of Mogadishu's Indian Ocean shorefront for Italian residents starting in the late 1930s. In the 1950s, under the postwar Italian trusteeship administration of Somalia, the beach was expanded again, anchored by a hotel that was briefly the tallest building in the city.

The beach outlived its colonial origin. Under President Siad Barre, who took power in a 1969 coup, Lido became the social landmark of the capital — women unveiled on the sand, beach clubs serving lunch on wooden decks a short walk from nightclubs that filled up after dark. It was, by multiple accounts of the period, where the city's cosmopolitan self-image was most visibly performed.

## The Ban

That performance stopped with the Somali state's collapse in 1991 and the clan warfare that followed. As Al-Shabab extended control over large parts of Mogadishu in the years after 2006, it enforced a strict reading of Islamic law that prohibited the kind of unsegregated public gathering Lido had always hosted. The beach that had defined the city's leisure identity sat largely unused under that occupation.

The occupation ended abruptly. On 6 August 2011, Al-Shabab withdrew its fighters from central Mogadishu under pressure from AMISOM and Somali government forces — a retreat, not a negotiated settlement. Within a short time, residents were back on the sand at Lido, and the beach again became a crowded weekend destination.

## Five Attacks, One Target

What came next was not a single tragedy but a pattern, repeated against the same roughly 500-meter stretch of coastline five times over eight years:

- **21 January 2016** — A suicide car bomb hit the gate of the Beach View Café, followed by gunmen who arrived partly by boat and fought government troops in a siege. Roughly 20 people were killed, 17 wounded.
- **16 August 2020** — Five Al-Shabab attackers detonated a car bomb and opened fire at the newly built Elite Hotel, owned by lawmaker Abdullahi Mohamed Nor. A four-hour siege ended with 16 dead, including the five attackers, and 43 wounded.
- **22 April 2022** — A suicide bomber, denied entry, detonated outside the recently opened Pescatore Seafood Restaurant during an Iftar gathering attended by the Somali police commissioner and members of parliament. Six people were killed.
- **9–10 June 2023** — Seven gunmen stormed the upmarket Pearl Beach Hotel after bombing its restaurant. Six civilians and three soldiers were killed, including a World Health Organization staff member, before security forces killed all seven attackers.
- **2 August 2024** — On a Friday night, when the beach was at its most crowded, a suicide bomber and gunmen hit the Beach View Hotel a second time. At least 32 people were killed and 63 wounded — the deadliest single attack in Mogadishu in nearly two years.

Every one of these attacks hit a hotel or restaurant, not a government or military installation. Somalia analyst Matt Bryden called the 2024 attack "a massive security failure," but the targeting itself reads less like a lapse than a message, repeated deliberately: the ordinary civilian life Mogadishu wants back — mixed crowds, unregulated leisure, diaspora visitors mingling with locals — is a target Al-Shabab considers legitimate. The beach's secular, civilian character is not incidental to the attacks. It is the reason for them.

## The Part That Isn't a Security Failure

What breaks the pattern is what happens afterward. Within a month of the August 2024 bombing, families were back on the sand watching the sunset. Guhad Mohamed, a 22-year-old survivor, told reporters that Lido was still "my favorite hangout place in Mogadishu; it's my hometown." Kassim Abdi, who lost his best friend Kamil Mohamed in the blast, said flatly that Al-Shabab's goal is to "instill fear and drive people away" — and came back anyway.

Some of that return is organized. A Friday-dawn cleanup that Abdisatar Arbow Ibrahim started with six volunteers grew to more than 300 participants by 2023, working the sand clear of trash before the day's crowds arrive — a civic effort built entirely outside any government or NGO program, on the same beach that keeps getting bombed.

## The Reckoning: Safety Isn't the Word for This

None of the return erases the deaths, and the danger has not stayed confined to Al-Shabab. On 27 February 2025, security personnel from Villa Somalia, the presidential compound, opened fire on civilians while clearing Lido ahead of a visit by Ethiopian Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed, killing a teenage boy who had not moved fast enough. The state apparatus built to protect the beach has, at least once, been the thing that killed someone on it.

Lido Beach today is not a place that has been made safe — the US State Department rates all of Somalia at Level 4, Do Not Travel, its highest category, and the hotel row still sits behind checkpoints and barriers heavier than anywhere else on the sand. What Lido's history actually shows is narrower and more specific: a nine-decade-old public beach that a militant group tried to erase twice, once by banning it and once by bombing it repeatedly, and that Mogadishu has, so far, refused to give up either time.
