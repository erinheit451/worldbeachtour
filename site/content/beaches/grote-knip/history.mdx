# Kenepa: The Plantation Behind the Postcard

Grote Knip's name is a straight line back to a plantation, and the plantation is where the largest slave revolt in Curaçao's history began. On 17 August 1795, an enslaved man named Tula and 40 to 50 others refused to report to work on the Kenepa estate and walked to confront its owner, Caspar Lodewijk van Uytrecht. What started as a work stoppage on one plantation grew, within days, into an uprising that swept across the western half of the island.

## What Happened, in Order

Tula's group did not stop at Kenepa. They moved on through the plantations of Lagún, Santa Cruz, Porto Marie, San Nicolas, Santa Martha, and San Juan, freeing enslaved people at each stop. By the time it peaked, the uprising numbered roughly 2,000 people — a scale that, on an island whose total population (free and enslaved) was a fraction of today's 156,000 residents, made it a genuine mass event, not an isolated protest.

Dutch colonial authorities took more than a month to regain control of the island. Tula was eventually captured. He was executed by torture on 3 October 1795, six weeks and change after he first refused to work.

## The Building That Still Stands

Landhuis Kenepa, the plantation's great house, sits about a kilometer inland from the beach. It is not visible from the sand and nothing at the beach marks the connection between the two. The house now operates as a museum devoted to Tula and the revolt, but even that institution has had gaps: the most recent update to the Dutch-language Wikipedia entry for the beach records that the museum closed in 2021 and reopened in 2024. For roughly three years, the one site that tells this story in detail was closed to the public while Grote Knip itself kept drawing rental-car day-trippers without interruption.

Curaçao does memorialize Tula, but the dedicated monument stands on the island's south coast — a separate location from Kenepa. A visitor who parks at Grote Knip, swims, and drives back to Willemstad has no on-site prompt to learn any of this.

## Why the Name Sticks

Curaçao's beaches are conventionally named for the nearest plantation, village, or landmark, so "Grote Knip" (Dutch for "Big Knip") is not an unusual naming pattern — it distinguishes this cove from Kleine Knip, the smaller one just to the south. What makes the pattern land differently here is what the plantation actually was: the site of an uprising serious enough that Dutch forces needed over a month to suppress it, and serious enough in scale — roughly 2,000 people — that it remains the largest event of its kind in Curaçao's history.

It is worth being precise about what is and is not established from the record: the trigger was Tula's refusal to work and a direct demand for freedom made to the plantation owner. What is not detailed in the available record is exactly what Van Uytrecht said or did in response before the group moved on to the next estate. The uprising's growth to roughly 2,000 people and its month-plus duration are the two figures that carry the most weight in assessing its scale.

## The Film That Carried It Further

In 2013, Dutch director Jeroen Leinders released *Tula: The Revolt*, a $4 million Dutch-Curaçaoan production. Obi Abili played Tula, with Danny Glover and Jeroen Krabbé in supporting roles. The film won the Grand Jury Prize at the 2013 London Film Awards and took Best Film and Best Lead Actor at the 2014 San Diego Black Film Festival. For most people outside Curaçao who have heard of Tula at all, the film — not the museum a kilometer from the beach — is the likely source.

## Geology Carries the Name Too

The connection between the beach's name and its history runs deeper than the sand. Curaçao's geology is grouped into four major named formations — a lava formation, a Mid-Curaçao formation, limestone formations, and the Knip formation, named for this same stretch of coast. The place name outlived the plantation economy that gave it to the land in the first place.

## What This Means for a Visit

There is no plaque at Grote Knip. There is no marker, no interpretive sign, and no indication on the sand that the cove takes its name from a plantation where a defining act of Dutch colonial-era resistance took place. If the history matters to a visit, it requires a deliberate detour: about a kilometer inland to Landhuis Kenepa, now open again as of 2024 after its 2021 closure. Everyone else gets the coral-rubble sand, the limestone walls, and a name that means nothing without the context most visitors will never seek out.
