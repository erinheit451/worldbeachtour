Grote Knip is a cove on Curaçao's west coast, in the Bandabou district between the villages of Westpunt and Lagún, about 35 km by road from Willemstad. The name is Dutch for "Big Knip," distinguishing it from Kleine Knip, the smaller cove just to the south; both derive from kenepa, the Papiamentu word for the genip tree, and from the Kenepa plantation that once stood roughly a kilometer inland. Locally the beach is also called Playa Abou, Papiamentu for "beach in a valley," describing how it sits wedged between limestone rock on both sides.

A narrow strip of coral rubble separates the sand from open water and marks the start of a reef, which is why the beach functions more as a snorkeling entry point than a swimming-only strand. The beach has thatched palapas over picnic tables, rows of rental sun loungers and umbrellas, and a small pavilion selling snacks intermittently, but nothing like a full supply point; the nearest real supply point is Westpunt village, a few kilometers north, population 738 as of the 2011 census.

## The Name Carries History

The plantation that gave the beach its name is also where the largest slave revolt in Curaçao's history began. On 17 August 1795, an enslaved man named Tula and 40 to 50 others walked off the Kenepa plantation to confront its owner and demand freedom; the uprising grew to roughly 2,000 participants before Dutch colonial forces suppressed it after more than a month. Tula was executed by torture on 3 October 1795. Landhuis Kenepa, the plantation house, still stands about a kilometer inland; it runs today as a museum, closed in 2021 and reopened in 2024. None of this is marked at the beach itself.

## Water and Weather

Open-Meteo's ERA5 marine reanalysis puts the average wave height at this stretch at 1.21 m across 2023-2024, biggest in February (1.36 m mean, up to 1.78 m) and calmest in October (0.99 m). The dataset's own summary is direct: "Exposed — real surf, and rip current to respect." Curaçao lies outside the Atlantic's main hurricane development region, and no hurricane has made direct landfall on the island since the U.S. National Hurricane Center began tracking storms, though systems still pass near it.
