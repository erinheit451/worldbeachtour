# The Sand That Ate a Lighthouse

Most beaches erode. L'Espiguette grows, and there is a 27-meter tower standing in the dunes that proves it. The Espiguette lighthouse was built in 1869, 150 meters from the Mediterranean. It now stands roughly 700 meters from the water, according to French Wikipedia's entry on the structure, which attributes the gap directly to "l'engraissement de la côte par le sable déposé par les courants" — the fattening of the coast by sand the currents deposit. That is not an estimate derived from erosion modeling. It is a fixed point, built in a known year, that the sea has been leaving behind ever since.

## Where the Sand Comes From

This dataset's own sediment sample for L'Espiguette puts the sand in a "mixed Q-F-L regime" — 55.3% quartz, 19.6% feldspar, and 25.1% lithic fragments (rock particles rather than single mineral grains), sourced from the GloPrSM global sand-provenance model. That mix is a signature of river-delivered sediment rather than a wave-sorted, quartz-dominant beach built from long-term coastal erosion. Pure quartz beaches form where waves grind down bedrock over centuries; a beach with a quarter lithic content is one still being actively resupplied by a river carrying mixed rock debris straight from an upstream catchment. Here, that river is the Rhône.

The Rhône delivers an estimated 20 million cubic meters of sediment to the Mediterranean every year. Longshore currents move a share of it westward and pile it at specific headlands rather than spreading it evenly along the coast. Espiguette is one of those headlands, along with the Pointe de Beauduc further east; French-language sources on the Camargue describe the beach reaching a kilometer in width at these points. That is the mechanism behind the lighthouse's drift from the shoreline: not subsidence, not a retreating sea, but sand arriving faster than it leaves.

## A Delta That Grows Here and Shrinks There

The same delta that grows at Espiguette is retreating elsewhere. Near Salin-de-Giraud and Saintes-Maries-de-la-Mer, the Camargue coast is losing ground, not gaining it. Sediment transport along a single delta coastline is a redistribution system: material that builds up at one point has often been pulled from, or simply never reached, another stretch of the same shore. And the total supply has been shrinking for decades. Dams built on the Rhône through the 20th century, for hydropower and flood control, trap sediment upstream that used to reach the delta. The river that built this entire landscape now delivers less material to it than it once did — which means Espiguette's growth is not proof the delta is thriving. It may be one of the last places still running a sediment surplus while the system as a whole runs a deficit.

The comparison that makes this legible without a measuring tape is Aigues-Mortes, the walled town 15 km inland whose 13th-century ramparts were built directly on the coast. The same accretion process that stranded the Espiguette lighthouse has left Aigues-Mortes roughly 5 kilometers from the sea, just over a longer timescale and a much greater distance. Two structures, two centuries apart in construction, both measuring the same delta doing the same thing.

## Sand as an Economic Fact, Not Just a Geological One

The sand here isn't only scenery. Fifteen kilometers up the coast toward Saintes-Maries-de-la-Mer, the Listel wine estate has grown vines directly in this belt of coastal sand since 1883. The sandy soil made the vineyard naturally immune to the phylloxera louse that devastated most of France's vines in the 1880s and 1890s — the insect cannot complete its life cycle in loose sand — which is why Listel's "Vin des Sables" survived a crisis that wiped out vineyards planted in ordinary soil a few kilometers inland. The same sediment system that buried the lighthouse's original shoreline also created, by accident of geology, one of France's few historically phylloxera-proof wine regions.

## What the Protection Actually Covers

The Pointe de l'Espiguette has been under formal protection since the Conservatoire du littoral, France's coastal land trust, began acquiring it in 1978; the site was classified a protected natural area in 1998. Today the Conservatoire controls roughly 525 hectares of dune and marsh here, inside a Natura 2000 zone that separately protects 8,970 hectares of marine sandbars offshore. The lighthouse itself was named a French historic monument in October 2012, transferred to the Conservatoire in February 2019, restored, and reopened to visitors in mid-2023.

None of that protection is decorative. The dunes inside it are actively accumulating the sand the designation exists to conserve, in real time, at a rate that — unusually for a natural process — is measurable against a specific 19th-century building. But the same 525 hectares also hold six NATO fuel storage tanks with a combined capacity of 66,000 cubic meters, installed in 1962 and still operated by the pipeline company Trapil as part of France's section of the alliance's Central European Pipeline System. The tanks have been there longer than any of the site's conservation designations. A beach that markets itself on wildness has held a military fuel depot in its dune hinterland for over sixty years; the sand and the storage tanks occupy the same protected ground without contradicting each other, which says something about how much "wild" actually means at a working stretch of European coastline.
