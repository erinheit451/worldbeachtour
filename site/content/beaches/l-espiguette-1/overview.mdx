# L'Espiguette: The Beach That Buried Its Own Lighthouse

L'Espiguette names two overlapping things on this stretch of the French Mediterranean: the Pointe de l'Espiguette, a dune promontory east of Le Grau-du-Roi in the Gard department, and the roughly 18-kilometer beach that runs along it to the mouth of the Petit Rhône. This page's coordinates sit at the point itself, near the 1869 lighthouse, not at the more developed beach access closer to town — worth flagging, since "L'Espiguette" alone is sometimes used loosely for either.

The clearest fact about this place is a measurement, not an impression. The Espiguette lighthouse was built 150 meters from the sea in 1869. It now stands roughly 700 meters inland, according to French Wikipedia's entry on the structure, stranded there by sand the Mediterranean's currents keep depositing along this stretch of coast. It is one of the few places in Europe where you can watch coastal change against a fixed, dated structure instead of a satellite time-lapse.

## What to Expect

L'Espiguette is undeveloped by design. The Conservatoire du littoral, France's coastal land trust, began buying the point in 1978 and had it classified a protected natural site by 1998; today it controls roughly 525 hectares of dune and marsh here. There are no beach clubs and no restaurants, and only the main RD255 access has a seasonal lifeguard post (2026: June 27–August 31, 11:00–18:30) — the rest of the beach is unsupervised. That RD255 road, renovated with a cycle path in 2011–2013, is the only paved access, ending in parking that's paid in season (roughly April–September, free for pedestrians and cyclists) and fills by mid-morning in July and August.

The beach itself is wide and flat, backed by dune systems rather than a promenade, with the lighthouse visible far up the sand at the point's southern, seaward end. A naturist stretch toward the eastern end has historically been associated with the Hélio-Marin association of Nîmes; family and clothed sections sit alongside it without any physical separation. Exposed to open Mediterranean swell, the beach also draws surfers and kitesurfers, particularly when the mistral is blowing.

## Most Relevant Lenses

**Sand and geology** — The measurable distance between the 1869 lighthouse and today's shoreline is the throughline for this entire page: a river delta building land in real time at one specific point, while other stretches of the same delta erode.

**Travel and access** — Getting here means driving; there's no rail line to the beach itself, parking is paid in season and unpaved, and there is genuinely nothing to buy once you're on the sand.

**Nature and protection** — The dune and marsh hinterland sits inside a Natura 2000 zone that also covers 8,970 hectares of offshore marine sandbars, though the same hinterland has held a NATO fuel depot since 1962.

## Practical Context

L'Espiguette rewards visitors who come for the landscape rather than for services: there's no food, no shade beyond the lighthouse, and no supervision in the water outside the seasonal lifeguard post at the main RD255 access. What it offers instead is 18 kilometers of open dune coastline and a lighthouse that, in itself, is a century and a half of coastal geology you can walk up to and touch.
