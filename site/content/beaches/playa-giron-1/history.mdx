# The Beach That Named a Battle: Girón, April 1961

Playa Girón is a fishing village of fewer than two thousand people on the east bank of the Bahía de Cochinos. In most of the world the events of April 1961 are called the Bay of Pigs invasion. In Cuba they are called Girón, after this beach, where the main seaborne landing came ashore and where, three days later, the invasion was destroyed. The history of the place is the history of those 72 hours and of how the Cuban state has kept them visible ever since.

## The plan and the ground

Brigade 2506 was a force of roughly 1,400 Cuban exiles, recruited, trained, and armed by the United States Central Intelligence Agency to overthrow Fidel Castro's government. The plan required air superiority, a secure beachhead, and a popular rising. The landing site chosen was the Zapata coast: a thin road running through the largest wetland in the Caribbean, with few approaches and two usable beaches. Playa Girón was the principal objective, code-named Blue Beach. Playa Larga, about 35 kilometres northwest up the bay, was Red Beach.

## The landing

Around midnight on 17 April 1961, two CIA landing craft, Blagar and Barbara J, led four transport ships, Houston, Río Escondido, Caribe, and Atlántico, toward the beaches. The brigade came ashore in the dark with five M41 tanks. It went wrong within hours. A pre-invasion air strike on 15 April had failed to destroy Castro's air force on the ground, so Cuban pilots still had aircraft, British-built Hawker Sea Fury propeller fighters and American-made T-33 jet trainers, in the air on the morning of the landing.

At about 09:30 on the 17th, a Sea Fury put a rocket into the Río Escondido, the transport carrying the brigade's reserve ammunition and its fuel for the tanks and aircraft. The ship exploded and sank. Near Playa Larga the Houston was hit and beached, and roughly 180 men who reached shore were out of the fight for lack of weapons. The brigade was now on the beaches with only what it had carried, and no way to resupply.

## Three days

Castro directed the response himself and moved militia, army, artillery, and tanks down the single swamp road toward the coast. The fighting spread through the Ciénaga de Zapata over about 72 hours, and it closed steadily on Girón, the last ground the brigade held. Without ammunition, and without the air cover and reinforcement the plan had assumed, the brigade's line broke. By 19 April 1961 the invasion had collapsed on this beach.

About 114 brigade members were killed in action, and roughly 1,189 were taken prisoner, many pulled out of the swamp over the following days. Cuban losses, among defenders and civilians, are recorded less precisely and are variously reported.

## The ransom and the aftermath

The prisoners became a long negotiation. After talks led by the American lawyer James B. Donovan, Cuba agreed to release the captured brigade in exchange for roughly $53 million in food and medicine; the bulk were returned to the United States in December 1962, with the remainder following through 1965. The defeat had lasting weight. It embarrassed the new Kennedy administration, hardened Castro's alliance with the Soviet Union, and helped set the stage for the Cuban Missile Crisis eighteen months later.

## Kept on the sand

What makes Girón unusual is that the battle was never allowed to fade into a plaque. The Museo Girón, founded in April 1976 and declared a National Monument in 1978, sits in the village. Parked outside it are a Hawker Sea Fury of the type that sank the Río Escondido, one of Brigade 2506's M41 tanks, and an anti-aircraft gun. Inside, two rooms hold photographs of the Cuban dead, captured uniforms and weapons, and field gear, with the brigade labelled in the captions as mercenaries. Along the coast road, individual concrete markers name Cuban fighters at the spots where they were killed, and a roadside billboard calls Girón the first defeat of imperialism in the Americas.

The name was also written into national institutions. The Order of Playa Girón, a state decoration, was created in 1961. The Campeonato Nacional de Boxeo Playa Girón, Cuba's national amateur boxing championship, has run under the beach's name since 1962, its champions including the three-time Olympic gold medallist Teófilo Stevenson.

That is what makes this shore worth a page. It is not the coral or the diving, real as both are. It is that a village of fewer than two thousand people became the shorthand for a superpower's most public failure in the hemisphere, and that you can still stand on the sand beside the tank and the fighter plane that decided it.
