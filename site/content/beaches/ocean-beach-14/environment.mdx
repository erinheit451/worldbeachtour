# The Sand Ocean Beach Can't Explain

San Francisco is committing $175 million to one of the largest seawalls in California history at the south end of Ocean Beach, meant to hold back an ocean that keeps taking sand from that stretch — without the city ever fully explaining why the sand goes missing there in the first place. That gap, between the scale of the fix and the depth of the understanding behind it, is the real environmental story at Ocean Beach, bigger than any single storm or any single dollar figure.

## A history of losing, and re-losing, the same sand

The erosion at Ocean Beach's south end is not a new problem. During the winter of 2009-10, storms hit the beach hard enough that the city spent $5 million on emergency remediation just to keep the shoreline and the infrastructure behind it intact. In 2021, the city tried a more direct fix: it spent $3.8 million bringing in sand from offshore sandbars to rebuild the beach. Waves swept most of that new sand away within months. Once, in 2021, the city explicitly paid to put new sand back — and lost most of it within months. Before that, the winter storms of 2009-10 had already cost the city $5 million in emergency erosion remediation. Twice, in other words, the city has paid to fight the same erosion, and neither fix has lasted.

## The plan that almost was

In 2012, planners floated the Ocean Beach Master Plan, a $350 million proposal that would have restored dune ecosystems, relocated some infrastructure inland, and used a smaller, buried seawall to protect a vulnerable sewage overflow tunnel. It was a plan built around working with the beach's natural movement rather than simply blocking it. That approach was largely set aside when the California Coastal Commission instead approved a $175 million plan in 2024 for a much larger, more conventional seawall at the same stretch of coast.

## What's actually getting built

The approved seawall would span the length of nine football fields and reach 55 feet in depth. What it protects is specific and unglamorous: the Oceanside Water Pollution Control Plant, which handles more than 12 million gallons of wastewater every day. Losing that plant to erosion, not losing sand for its own sake, is the scenario the seawall is designed to prevent. As of a mid-2026 project update from the SFPUC, the timeline for major construction has slipped: an earlier target of 2027 has been pushed back to late 2029, with the agency citing needed safety work at the Sloat Boulevard/SF Zoo intersection and ongoing coordination with the California Coastal Commission.

## A coastline under longer-term pressure

None of this is happening in isolation. The U.S. Geological Survey has projected that 25% to 70% of California's coastline could disappear by 2100 if greenhouse gas emissions are not changed. Ocean Beach's shifting sand, in that light, is a local, urgent version of a statewide question that doesn't have a settled answer either: how much of the California coast is worth engineering around, and how much will simply be lost.

## The life that depends on this shoreline anyway

The same stretch of coast the city is racing to armor also supports real, federally protected wildlife. The western snowy plover, listed as a federally threatened species by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service in 1993, rests at Ocean Beach for 10 months of the year. Protecting it is largely a matter of asking dog owners to leash their pets in the plover zone — a rule that depends entirely on voluntary compliance. A Golden Gate Audubon Society survey covering 2009-2010 found only about 35% compliance with the leash requirement. A National Park Service survey covering 2019-2020 still found 70% of pets running off-leash. The rule exists on paper; on the sand, most owners still don't follow it.

The beach's ecology is also shifting in ways that track the erosion story. Ocean Beach is often blanketed with sand dollars of the species Dendraster excentricus, a sign of a living intertidal zone even as the sand itself is contested ground. And in recent decades, bottlenose dolphins — traditionally a Southern California and Baja California species — have moved north into these waters as ocean temperatures have risen, the same warming trend that helps drive the erosion debate in the first place.

## What the mystery actually is

Put together, the story is not that Ocean Beach's south end is disappearing — that part is well documented, from the 2009-10 storm damage through the 2021 sand replenishment that washed away within months. The story is that after two expensive, short-lived fixes and a discarded $350 million alternative plan, the city's answer is a $175 million, nine-football-field seawall aimed at protecting a wastewater plant, on a timeline that keeps slipping, on a stretch of coast where a federally protected shorebird spends ten months a year and most dog owners still don't leash their pets. The seawall may well work. What's missing from the public record is a clear account of why the sand behaves the way it does here, distinct from anywhere else along this same beach — and until that gap closes, every dollar spent is a bet against a mechanism nobody has fully described.

## What this means if you're standing on the sand

None of this is visible to someone walking the beach on an ordinary day. The plover protection signage, the fire rings, the fog — those are the surface. Underneath is a shoreline the city has spent tens of millions of dollars fighting to hold in place, twice failed to hold with cheaper methods, and is now betting $175 million on holding with a much bigger one, on a schedule that has already slipped once. Visitors don't need to track any of that to enjoy Ocean Beach. But the ordinary, photogenic version of the beach — dunes, dogs, sand dollars, a good sunset — sits directly on top of an engineering and ecology problem the public record has not fully solved, and that gap is worth knowing about even if it changes nothing about how you spend an afternoon there.
