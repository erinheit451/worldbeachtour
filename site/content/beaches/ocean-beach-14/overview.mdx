# Ocean Beach

Ocean Beach is the 3.5-mile Pacific-facing shoreline that forms San Francisco's western edge. It runs from the Cliff House and Sutro Baths area in the north down to Fort Funston in the south, paralleled for most of its length by the corridor that used to carry the Great Highway. The National Park Service administers it as part of the Golden Gate National Recreation Area. Surfers have long called the northern stretch, near the Cliff House, Kelly's Cove — a name that shows up on local maps and in surf reports more often than "Ocean Beach" does for that particular section.

## What kind of beach this is

This is not a sun-and-swim beach. The water is cold year-round, the beach faces the open Pacific with nothing sheltering it, and the currents are genuinely dangerous — they move at very different speeds along the shoreline — fastest near the inlet throat, calmer at the north end, and slowest of all at the south end — so a stretch that looks calm can sit close to a stretch that isn't. Fog is a defining, constant presence rather than an occasional inconvenience. People come to Ocean Beach to walk, to run on firm sand, to watch surfers, and to photograph fog rolling across the dunes — not, generally, to swim.

## Who actually goes there

Surfers are the most consistent presence, particularly around Kelly's Cove, where the beach has hosted world-championship-level competition. Photographers come for the interplay of fog and light, especially near the Cliff House ruins at the northern end. Walkers and runners use the sand itself, and dog owners use the beach heavily enough that a federal shorebird-protection rule there depends specifically on their cooperation. Families looking for a conventional beach day — calm water, easy swimming, food stands — are better served elsewhere in the Bay Area; Ocean Beach's appeal is the raw version of the Pacific, not a managed one.

## Life on the sand

Ocean Beach is often blanketed with sand dollars of the species Dendraster excentricus, visible evidence of an active intertidal zone. In recent decades, bottlenose dolphins — traditionally a Southern California and Baja California species — have moved north into these waters as ocean temperatures have risen, joining a beach ecosystem that also supports the western snowy plover, a federally threatened shorebird that rests here for most of the year.

## What's changed most recently

The biggest recent change at Ocean Beach wasn't to the sand — it was to the road running alongside it. In 2025, San Francisco permanently closed the stretch of Great Highway along the beach and replaced it with a new park, Sunset Dunes, a move that turned out to be far more popular with the public than it was survivable for the supervisor who championed it. The full story, including what happened to him and to the highway's opponents, is on [the main Ocean Beach guide](/beaches/ocean-beach-14). The separate, ongoing fight over the eroding sand at the beach's south end and the seawall proposed to fix it is covered in [the environment deep dive](/beaches/ocean-beach-14/environment).
