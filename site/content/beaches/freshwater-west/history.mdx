# The Range Behind the Beach: Castlemartin and the Bundeswehr Years

Freshwater West's global reputation runs through a house-elf's grave. Its more consequential history runs through a tank range that a foreign army used to rebuild itself after losing a world war. Both sit on the same few hundred meters of Pembrokeshire coast, and only one of them is still active.

## A Range Built From Farmland and a Defunct Estate

Castlemartin Training Area was established in 1938 out of a mix of deserted and inhabited farmland and parts of the defunct Cawdor Estate — a roughly 6,000-acre tract immediately inland of Freshwater West, between the beach and the village of Castlemartin. The Royal Armoured Corps used it for tank gunnery through the Second World War. Like many British ranges, it went quiet soon after 1945, then was reinstated in 1951 once the Korean War revived demand for large-scale tank training ground.

## The Arrangement With West Germany

The distinctive chapter starts in 1961. West Germany had joined NATO in 1955 and was rebuilding an army — the Bundeswehr — largely from scratch. By 1961 there was a shortage of suitable tank-training ground in the northern part of West Germany for the newly reactivated German tank units, and the British Army of the Rhine, already stationed in West Germany, could not absorb the additional demand on its own UK ranges. NATO brokered an agreement in Paris: West German tank units could use Castlemartin.

From May 1961, German soldiers were firing live tank rounds on a range a few hundred meters from a beach that would, decades later, become known internationally for a scene involving a fictional elf. The arrangement was not brief or symbolic. It ran continuously for 35 years, until October 1996, ending only after German reunification gave the newly unified Bundeswehr access to ranges in the former East Germany — territory that, six years earlier, had not been available to any West German unit at all.

## What "Active 44 Weeks a Year" Actually Means

Castlemartin never stopped being a working range after the Germans left. The British Army describes it as the country's only range generally available for large-scale live-fire gunnery and manoeuvre training, and it operates roughly 44 weeks out of 52. When firing is underway, offshore exclusion zones extend anywhere from 3 to 12 nautical miles, depending on the weapon system and ammunition in use — a hazard radius that, at its widest, reaches past Freshwater West itself and into the water surfers use for the beach's Atlantic swell.

The Pembrokeshire Coast Path, which would otherwise run directly along this stretch of cliff, has been permanently diverted inland because of the range. In 2011 the Ministry of Defence opened a dedicated diversionary route so walkers no longer had to negotiate the narrow country roads with fast-moving traffic that the previous route required — a safety upgrade for pedestrians rather than a change driven by the range itself.

## The Deaths Are Recent, Not Historical

It would be easy to file all of this under Cold War trivia — interesting, but settled. It isn't. Three fatal training accidents have occurred at Castlemartin in the twenty-first century. Ranger Michael Maguire of the Royal Irish Regiment was killed by a stray round in May 2012. Two Royal Tank Regiment crew members were killed in June 2017 when an incompletely fitted tank part allowed hot gas to ignite weapon charges. Sergeant Gavin Hillier of the Welsh Guards died during a live-firing exercise in March 2021. None of these deaths happened on the public beach. All of them happened on a range that shares a fence line with it, close enough that the coast path's inland diversion and the beach's own safety notices exist partly because of what happens on the other side of that boundary.

## Two Film Crews Used the Same Emptiness, Differently

The isolation that made Castlemartin useful to the military — remote, sparsely populated, easy to secure — is also what made Freshwater West useful to film productions that wanted an empty coastline for the opposite reason. In June and July 2009, Ridley Scott's *Robin Hood* filmed a beach battle here depicting the French invasion of southern England, with over 600 extras and 150 horses on the sand. That same year, Warner Bros. built a temporary Shell Cottage in the dunes for *Harry Potter and the Deathly Hallows – Part 1*, the set for Dobby the house-elf's death and burial. The cottage was fully dismantled once filming wrapped, leaving nothing of the set on site. The grave visitors find in the dunes is a fan-made memorial built at the filming spot, and on 1 November 2022 the National Trust for Wales announced it could remain in the immediate term, asking visitors to take only photographs and leave no painted pebbles or socks.

Neither film production needed the range next door. Both needed the same absence of development that the range's presence had helped preserve for over 70 years — a beach kept empty first by farmland and a defunct estate, then by the Ministry of Defence, and, almost incidentally, by two national governments deciding in 1961 that this stretch of Wales was where a reconstituted German army should learn to fire tank rounds.

## The Layering, Summed Up

Freshwater West's history is not one narrative with the military range as a footnote or the film locations as the headline. It is several unrelated institutions occupying the same physical space for reasons that never had anything to do with each other: British farmland requisitioned in 1938, a NATO accommodation for a rearming ally in 1961, a Hollywood production's need for emptiness in 2009, and a national surfing federation's need for a reliable Atlantic break, all layered onto roughly the same mile of Pembrokeshire sand. The range is still active. The film set is gone. The layering is what's left standing.
