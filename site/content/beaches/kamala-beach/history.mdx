# From Fishing Village to Bondholders' Meeting: Kamala's Short, Documented History

Kamala Beach has existed as a place name for longer than anyone bothered to write down, but its documented history is short and unusually well-dated: a fishing village into the early 1990s, the first hotels around 1994, a $100-million theme park in 1998, a tsunami in 2004, and a bond restructuring filed with Thailand's securities regulator in 2024. Few 2-kilometer stretches of sand have that much of their recent past on the public record.

## Before the Resorts

Travel writers who visited Kamala in the early 1990s describe a fishing settlement, not a destination: buffaloes on the sand, fishermen drying small fish on tarps and in baskets, a scatter of thatched huts, a school, and a temple. The area's Sanskrit- and Pali-derived name, kamalā, means "lotus." The first hotels appeared around 1994, and development accelerated from there, but the older village never disappeared. It survives inland, organized around the marble-fronted Kamala Mosque on Khok Yang street, and multiple independent tourism and real-estate sources still describe Kamala as effectively two places sharing one beach: a Muslim fishing community set back from the water, and a resort strip built along it. The subdistrict's own administrative registry counted 5,459 residents across six villages as of 31 March 2004 — a snapshot from just before the tsunami that would define the next chapter.

## The $100 Million Bet at the North End

Phuket FantaSea Public Company Limited opened its gates on 20 December 1998, at the northern tip of Kamala's beach, after spending more than 3,500 million baht — roughly $100 million at the time — on a 60-acre nighttime cultural theme park. The centerpiece, "Fantasy of a Kingdom," is a 70-minute stage show blending illusion effects with Thai cultural performance, staged inside the Palace of the Elephants, a three-story reproduction of Sukhothai-era stone architecture that seats 3,000. Next door, the Golden Kinnaree Buffet Restaurant covers 5,220 square meters and seats up to 4,000 diners, sized among the largest buffet halls documented anywhere. A Festival Village shopping street rounds out the site. It was built for a tourism market that, in 1998, looked like it would only keep growing.

## The Tsunami, and the Recovery That Worked

On 26 December 2004, the Indian Ocean tsunami reached Phuket's west coast, including Kamala. A surviving amateur video shot at Kamala — preserved in tsunami-reconnaissance archives rather than any tourism record — shows the ground floor of a beachfront restaurant flooding and sweeping away an elderly couple. Thailand recorded roughly 8,000 tsunami deaths that day, most of them around Khao Lak rather than Phuket's resort beaches; Kamala's damage was real but not the region's worst.

FantaSea, set back from the open shoreline, still absorbed an estimated 60 to 70 million baht in physical damage and a heavier 150 to 200 million baht in lost bookings, as tour operators cancelled reservations through the following February — normally peak season. Daily visitor counts fell from a typical 3,000 to 6,000 down to as few as 15. Managing director Pin Kewkacha closed the park on 10 January 2005, in what was announced as a ten-day closure to 18 January, and reopened on Wednesday, 19 January 2005 — nine days later than the original 10-day estimate — with about 100 visitors at the first show, mostly foreign tourists, continuing to operate from there while pushing publicly for government-subsidized package tours to rebuild demand. The park did eventually recover: it operated for the next two decades without a comparable disaster closure, even if that January reopening was not the clean ten-day turnaround sometimes described. The 2004 shock was survivable because it was a single, sharp event with an end date.

## The Slower Shock: Bonds Rolled Over Since the Pandemic

The crisis that has occupied FantaSea's finances since the pandemic is a different kind of problem — not a wave, but a balance sheet. Phuket FantaSea Public Company Limited (SET: PHUKET), which operates the park, has been rolling over six separate bond series, originally due in 2020-2021, through successive bondholder-approved extensions: PHUKET206A, PHUKET207A, PHUKET207B, PHUKET208A, PHUKET20DA, and PHUKET216A, layered on top of an existing 2021 debt-restructuring debenture. Its parent, Safari World Public Company, which trades on the Stock Exchange of Thailand under the symbol SAFARI, enters the picture only as the potential recipient if the PHUKET216A series converts to equity. On 4 December 2024, Thailand's Securities and Exchange Commission issued a public notice urging bondholders to attend a meeting five days later in Bangkok, to vote on extending the debt's 30 December 2024 maturity by eighteen months, to 30 June 2026, with interest reset across three components: a 7.25 percent normal rate, 5.25 percent on remaining prior-period interest, and an additional 6.25 percent paid in three installments through 2025.

That is not routine seasonal financing language. It is the language of a company that could not meet its debt obligations on the original schedule and needed creditor consent to avoid default. The park did not close. Its social pages still carried five-figure follower counts, and travel sites kept listing it among Kamala's top attractions. But by April 2026, FantaSea's own published schedule showed "Fantasy of a Kingdom" running three nights a week — Tuesday, Friday, and Sunday — instead of the near-nightly cadence a 3,000-seat theater and 4,000-seat buffet hall were built to sustain.

The 30 June 2026 fix didn't hold either. A second bondholders' meeting, called for 15 December 2025, asked holders to push the maturity another year, to 30 June 2027, raise the rate from 7.25 to 7.50 percent effective 30 December 2025, waive default provisions to allow restructuring talks with creditors, and consider converting the PHUKET216A series to equity — against 2,288.7 million baht in principal still outstanding as of 1 December 2025.

## What the Sources Get Wrong

Not every claim about Kamala's history checks out. Kamala Beach's own Wikipedia article carries a formal "unreliable sources" flag, dated August 2021, attached to a section describing the beach as a regional center for illegal cockfighting breeding. Tracing the article's own citations shows the underlying police-raid reports describe operations in Wichit and Pa Khlok — other parts of Phuket, not Kamala. It's a small case study in how a specific, colorful claim about a place can spread without ever being tied to the place it's attached to, and it's why that claim is left out of this account rather than repeated as settled fact.

## The Line Through It

Kamala's fishing-village core is still there, still organized around its own mosque and market, largely untouched by the numbers above. But the built landmark that defines the beach to outsiders — the elephant palace, the 4,000-seat buffet hall, the nightly show — is also the clearest documented case of how thin the margin can be between a landmark Thai tourist attraction and a company managing default risk. In 1998 that margin looked wide. By 2024 it was the subject of a securities filing.
