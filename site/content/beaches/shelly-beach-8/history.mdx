# The Century Shelly Beach Almost Lost

Shelly Beach's history is less a story about a beach than a story about who got to decide what a beach was for. Twice across more than a century, the public's access to this small cove at Manly was contested — first by a private exclusion, then by a 1920s redevelopment scheme — before the state itself eventually decided to protect the water rather than develop it.

## Shut out, then let back in

Shelly Beach and its ocean foreshore were excluded from public access in 1885. It took until 1898 for that to change, and it changed only because Manly Council asked for it back, on the grounds that it was already such a popular recreation spot. That is the first data point in a pattern that repeats through the beach's history: public use of Shelly Beach has never been a given, and it has been won back, not simply granted, more than once.

Even while that access dispute played out, the beach was already a working part of the community. The Sly family kept fishing boats at Shelly Beach from 1888, and by 1903 were patrolling the water with their own boat, an early, informal demonstration of surf rescue methods well before any organised lifesaving service existed here. Cabbage Tree Bay itself was, for decades, an important commercial fishing ground — up to fifty years ago, huge schools of kingfish, salmon and tuna were still being netted in these waters, a use of the bay that the modern no-take reserve has since made illegal.

## The amusement park that didn't happen

In the 1920s, developers proposed converting the Manly foreshore — Shelly Beach included — into an amusement park. The surviving record doesn't spell out what rides or attractions were planned; what it does make clear is that local opposition was strong enough to kill the scheme outright. That single act of resistance is credited, in the same account, with eventually helping Cabbage Tree Bay become a no-take aquatic reserve — a direct line from "developers turned away" to "marine sanctuary," even if the legal protection itself didn't arrive for decades.

It's worth sitting with how differently this could have gone. Sydney has plenty of foreshore given over to exactly the kind of built recreation the 1920s scheme proposed — piers, pavilions, fairground attractions. Shelly Beach's underwater life today — the blue gropers, the seagrass meadows, the small colony of hard coral establishing itself as tropical species drift south — exists specifically because that alternative future was refused by the people who lived here, not because anyone planned a marine reserve in 1920.

The same decade gave the beach one of its few surviving structures. Shelly Beach's present stone kiosk began life as a tearoom, built in 1923, and it hosted community dances that same decade — a modest, still-standing counterpoint to the amusement park that was never built. By 1933, though, the beach's own namesake resource had thinned out: the huge quantities of shells the tide once deposited regularly had mostly been carried off by visitors, to the point that hundreds were reported taken and few remained.

## A rare find, and a working dive centre

Shelly Beach's strangest recorded moment came in 1936, when G.J. Sly found an extremely rare vampire octopus washed up on the sand — one of only ten ever sighted anywhere in the world, and the only one ever found in Australia. It was pink, about 1.25 metres across, and its scientific significance did not save it from an undignified end: it was used as bait.

Diving itself became a fixture of the local economy in 1962, when a dive shop opened on Belgrave Street. Under different names and owners — Dive Centre Manly from 1993, Dive Sydney since 2023 — a dive business has served the Manly community from that address ever since, one of the reasons scuba schools still treat Shelly Beach as a default first-dive location: the shallow, sheltered water here has offered beginner-friendly conditions for longer than most of the reserve's protections have existed.

## From informal shore to enforced reserve

Protection, when it finally came, arrived in stages rather than all at once. The rocky shore of Cabbage Tree Bay was designated an intertidal protected area starting in 1993 — nine years before the water itself got the same treatment. The full "no-take" aquatic reserve was declared on 31 March 2002, closing off fishing, spearing and collecting across roughly 20 hectares of bay, rocky shore, reef, seagrass meadow and kelp forest. It is that declaration, not the 1920s fight alone, that turned a stretch of coast people had twice had to fight to use into a place actively managed for what lives in the water rather than for what could be built beside it.

The reserve has needed defending in more literal ways too. A severe storm on 4-5 June 2016 badly damaged the walkway between Manly Beach and Shelly Beach, including the sculpture at Fairy Bower Pool; safety barriers went up immediately afterward and weren't removed until August 2016, once the repair work was finished. It was a reminder that "protected" doesn't mean "unchanging" — the same coastline that survived a century of access fights still answers to storms on its own schedule.

## What the fight actually bought

None of this history erases Shelly Beach's more recent, less flattering headlines — a 2025 water-quality report that ranked it among Sydney's worst swim sites, and two dated, real shark incidents in the surrounding water. Those belong to the present, not the past, and they're addressed honestly elsewhere on this page rather than smoothed over here.

What the century of access fights and slow-motion legal protection did buy is more basic: a beach that still exists in a form recognisable to the people who fought for it in 1898 and again in the 1920s, rather than one redeveloped past the point of recognition. The amusement park was the closest call. Everything since — the kiosk, the dive centre, the reserve declaration — happened because that one proposal didn't.
