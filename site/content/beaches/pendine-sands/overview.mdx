# Pendine Sands

Pendine Sands is a beach on Carmarthen Bay, on the south coast of Carmarthenshire, Wales, known in Welsh as Traeth Pentywyn. It runs west to east from Gilman Point; Wikipedia names the eastern end Laugharne Sands, while Pendine Community Council's own materials call it Ginst Point instead — the two disagree, and this page does not pick a side. The village of Pendine, Welsh Pentywyn ("end of the dunes"), sits at the beach's western end and had a population of 346 at the 2011 census. The beach and the village are distinct: no part of the open sand belongs to the village simply because it shares the name.

## What It Is

Pendine is known internationally for a short, intense run of land speed record attempts made here between 1924 and 1927, and for the car at the center of the story — Parry-Thomas's Babs, mutilated and buried in the dunes after his fatal 1927 crash, later dug up and restored. The full story, including what happened in the decades the car spent underground, is in the History deep dive linked from this page.

## Who Owns It, and Who Goes

Ownership is split and unequal. Pendine Community Council owns and manages the section at the village end and to the west; the Ministry of Defence, through its contractor QinetiQ, owns the larger section to the east and normally closes it to the public on weekdays. The MOD's range covers 20.5 km² with 9 km of shoreline and three test tracks, land the MOD acquired in 1940 to develop wartime weapons and train troops for the Normandy landings. That history still shapes who can go where, and when.

West of the village, the cliffs and rock pools sit within the Marros-Pendine Coast Site of Special Scientific Interest, designated in 1988 and covering 2.5 km² of shoreline and cliff. Predicted sand composition on the beach is lithic-rich — roughly a third quartz, well under a tenth feldspar, and most of the rest rock-fragment grains — a mineral mix more typical of a volcanic or collision-zone coast than an ordinary quartz-sand strand.

## Present-Day Pendine

The beach's more recent public life runs through film and museums rather than racing. It has stood in as a location for BBC's Top Gear and the BBC Cymru drama Keeping Faith, and in 2011 Aardman Animations built what it called the world's largest stop-motion film set here for the short film Gulp. The original Pendine Museum of Speed, open from 1996 and visited by 33,522 people in 2009, was demolished in 2021; a new Museum of Land Speed has since opened on the promenade, its construction decided on in 2018 at a cost of £7 million.

Visitors tend to fall into a few groups: motorsport and aviation history enthusiasts drawn by the Blue Bird and Babs story, walkers and photographers using the beach's length and openness, and people combining a stop here with the Marros-Pendine Coast cliffs or the wider Wales coastline. Anyone hoping to drive, sail, or launch a boat on the beach needs to plan around MOD hours and club membership rules covered in the Travel guide.

Who goes: land-speed history followers, coastal walkers and photographers, and — on the days it is open to them — members of registered boating and land-sailing clubs. Who should think twice: anyone expecting an always-open beach with visitor facilities on the sand itself; there are none directly on it.
