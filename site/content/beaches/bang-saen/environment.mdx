# Whales, a Dead Dolphin, and Black Water: Bang Saen's Marine Story

Bang Saen Beach markets itself as a calm, sheltered stretch of the Gulf of Thailand -- a place where Bangkok weekenders swim without the surf or currents of Thailand's exposed coasts. Over a seven-month stretch from November 2025 to June 2026, the water off that beach also turned visibly black, stranded and killed a marine mammal that had never been recorded there before, and hosted what may be the biggest documented gathering of whales the area has seen. None of it happened because anyone was watching for it. All of it was reported after the fact, by vendors, rescue teams, and boaters who happened to be there.

## The Whales

On June 26, 2026, tourists and boaters near Bang Saen Beach documented a pod of more than 13 Bryde's whales, including calves, swimming and feeding about 2 kilometers offshore in Saen Suk Subdistrict -- the highest count researchers had recorded that year, more than the year before. The largest individual weighs over a ton, roughly the size of a city bus. Researchers do not just count the whales; they identify individuals by their scars, spot patterns, and barnacle growth, and they track two distinct groupings in these waters: one near Koh Sampanyue, north of Koh Si Chang, and a second closer to shore near Khao Sam Muk and Ang Sila, the headland and fishing town that bracket Bang Saen.

Bryde's whales are baleen whales, and a pod of this size persisting this close to a working municipal beach reads as a genuine wildlife recovery in the upper Gulf of Thailand -- a body of water more associated with cargo shipping lanes and commercial fishing fleets than with megafauna. But the recovery comes with a documented threat. Conservationists have specifically flagged nighttime vessel traffic, particularly from commercial fishing boats, as a risk to the pod. The mechanism is physiological: Bryde's whales rest with only half their brain asleep at a time, alternating hemispheres so they can keep swimming and keep surfacing to breathe even while resting. It is an adaptation for staying alive at sea. It is not an adaptation for spotting a hull in the dark.

## The Dolphin

Six weeks before the whale pod was counted, the same general stretch of water produced a smaller, sadder story. On May 15, 2026, a baby Irrawaddy dolphin -- about 1.20 meters long -- stranded in the surf at Bang Saen Beach. Saen Suk's White Shark Marine Life Rescue Team, led by Natdanai Mongkunkong, pulled it out and, in the team's own words, encountered a stray Irrawaddy dolphin calf at Bang Saen for the first time ever. The calf was taken to Burapha University's Institute of Marine Science -- the same institution whose researchers study the offshore whale pod -- for round-the-clock veterinary care. It died despite the effort.

Irrawaddy dolphins are typically associated with river mouths, estuaries, and brackish coastal water, not open swimming beaches on the Gulf proper. A calf ending up disoriented enough to strand itself at Bang Saen is, on its own, an anomaly. Paired with the whale pod's arrival a year later, it reads less like an isolated tragedy and more like a signal that this particular stretch of Gulf water is busier with marine life -- and with the vessel traffic that shares it -- than the beach's calm, family-friendly image suggests.

## The Black Water

Seven months before the dolphin died, on November 7, 2025, Bang Saen's shoreline water turned visibly black. The account of what happened, and why, came entirely from the people standing on the sand. Sirikanya Triudom, a 70-year-old beach vendor, described the change directly: two weeks earlier, at the start of the cool season, the water had been crystal clear; then rain and freshwater runoff from the Bang Pakong River began mixing with the seawater, and it started looking like this. Phuridech Singsat, a 30-year-old food vendor, offered the reassurance that stood in for any formal water-quality reading: it's not dangerous, there are no dead fish, there's no strong smell, it just looks odd. Vendors expected the color to clear within days.

No dissolved-oxygen reading, no bacterial count, and no government water-quality report accompanied any of this. A Pollution Control Department report and a Bangkok Post story alleging low dissolved-oxygen levels and roughly 10 tons of garbage a year did surface in search results during research for this page, but neither could be independently fetched or verified -- the Bangkok Post link returned a paywall error and the Pollution Control Department's site returned a connection error -- so those specific figures are not repeated here. What is verifiable is narrower and more human: two vendors, reacting in real time, were the public record of what happened to the water that day.

## What Connects Them

Institutionally, these three events share an address. Burapha University's Institute of Marine Science sits beside Bang Saen Beach and runs an aquarium that is itself a major tourist attraction, independent of the sand. It is the same institute that treated the stranded dolphin calf and that researchers use to study the whale pod's individuals. That concentration of expertise is a genuine asset -- Bang Saen has, on its doorstep, one of the few institutions positioned to track exactly the kind of marine event that keeps happening here.

What the beach doesn't have, based on what is documented, is a standing public monitoring or reporting system that connects those events for visitors before the fact rather than after it. No official source confirms lifeguard staffing, rip-current risk assessments, or a formal water-quality alert protocol specific to Bang Saen. The black water was explained by vendors. The dolphin's stranding was explained by a rescue team already in motion. The whale pod was explained by boaters who happened to be looking at the right moment. Each account is credible on its own terms. None of them adds up to a system watching the water in advance.

That gap doesn't make Bang Saen dangerous. The wave data is calm -- an annual mean height under half a meter, biggest in April at 0.55 m, calmest in October at 0.14 m -- and there is no record of shark incidents here. But it does mean that anyone treating Bang Saen's marine environment as simply "safe, sheltered Gulf water" is working from the brochure description, not from the seven months of record this page just walked through. The whales are worth seeing. The water that carries them is worth watching, too, and right now that watching is being done informally, by whoever happens to be on the beach.
