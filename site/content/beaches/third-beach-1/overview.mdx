Third Beach is a public swimming beach at Ferguson Point, on the western side of Stanley Park in Vancouver, British Columbia. It is a naturally sandy beach -- not the pebble-and-coarse-sand shoreline some older write-ups describe -- built up over the twentieth century by the Vancouver Park Board, which dredged sand in from English Bay. Old-growth forest presses in close behind the sand, cutting the beach off from the rest of the city more than its distance from downtown would suggest.

Ferguson Point itself has a history that has nothing to do with swimming. A World War II coastal gun battery once stood where the beach's own parking lot now sits, built over the objections of Vancouver's own planning commission, which called the site "a favourite beauty spot in the park." The full account -- construction, occupation, and the fight to get the land back -- is in the [history deep dive](/beaches/third-beach-1/history).

North along the seawall, past the beach, stands Siwash Rock: a basalt sea stack that mariners nicknamed Nine Pin Rock for its resemblance to a bowling pin, and that carries a Squamish name tied to a transformation legend recorded in Pauline Johnson's *Legends of Vancouver*. A Vancouver Park Board commissioner has moved to make that Squamish name the rock's official one.

On-site amenities are limited but real: a concession stand, public washrooms, barbecues subject to restrictions, and lifeguards on duty from Victoria Day to Labour Day. Paid parking runs along the Stanley Park seawall system. Water-quality reporting for the beach is handled by Swim Drink Fish -- Vancouver, formerly known as Fraser Riverkeeper, which posts sample results through the sampling season.

The beach's other identity is cultural: every Tuesday evening from May through September, a drum circle called Brahm's Tams gathers near Ferguson Point, a tradition organizers describe as one of the city's most photographed weekly summer gatherings. It has also drawn safety scrutiny of its own, covered in full on the [history spoke](/beaches/third-beach-1/history) alongside the site's military past.

Third Beach's identity, in short, is layered: a sandy, forest-backed swimming spot built on land the city fought a war department to get back, next to a landmark rock still carrying a contested name, with a weekly cultural gathering that has needed its own safety response.
