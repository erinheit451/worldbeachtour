# The Testimonial That Built Doctor's Cave

Doctor's Cave Beach is named for a real doctor and made famous by a claim no doctor ever proved. Dr. Alexander James McCatty founded a sanatorium in Montego Bay in 1880 and let physician friends bathe at his small beach, reached through a cave; in 1906 he donated the beach and the club formed. That donation, not the sanatorium's 1880 opening, is the date the club counts as its founding. Two decades later, an English surgeon's magazine article about the water's "curative" properties, a claim that was never clinically tested, turned the club into an international destination and helped build Montego Bay's identity as a health-tourism town. That history is still legible in how the beach operates today: a private, fee-gated cove charging admission for water that sits inside a public marine park.

## The Doctor Who Donated the Cove

McCatty founded a sanatorium in Montego Bay in 1880 and let physician friends bathe at his small beach, reached through a cave, alongside him regularly. In 1906 he donated the beach outright, and that circle of doctors formalized the arrangement as a private members' bathing club. The name is a literal description, not a marketing invention: the earliest people to swim here, by the club's own account and Wikipedia's, were a group of doctors.

## The Article That Made It Famous

For close to two decades the club stayed a local curiosity. What changed that was a visit from Herbert Barker, an English manipulative surgeon whose own biographical record places him in a discipline distinct from mainstream osteopathy, though period writing about Jamaica called him an osteopath regardless. In the 1920s Barker wrote an article praising the cove's water, and it landed at almost the exact moment transatlantic steamship travel was making the Caribbean newly reachable for wealthy North American and European tourists. A single doctor's endorsement, printed for an English-reading audience, is credited with turning a private swimming hole into an internationally known destination. No clinical study has ever backed the curative claim. What sold the beach in the 1920s, and arguably still sells it, was a testimonial rather than a diagnosis.

## A Century of Paying at the Gate

The exclusivity built into that origin story never really left. Doctor's Cave has operated as a members' club with paid day access for more than a century, in a country where a meaningful share of the coastline nearest the major resort towns is privately controlled, whether by hotels or, in this case, by a legacy bathing club. Current admission, checked against the club's own site in July 2026, is $8 for adults and $4 for children under 12, for hours running 8:30 a.m. to 5:30 p.m., a window that closes before local sunset for most of the year. In recent years the club has framed that fee partly as conservation funding: it has backed Montego Bay Marine Park's operations since 2017, including paying for a patrol boat engine, and has partnered with Recycling Partners of Jamaica on plastic waste since 2023. Both contributions are real and independently documented. Neither changes who is asked to pay at the gate.

## The Park Built Around It

The water McCatty and Barker praised now sits inside Montego Bay Marine Park, Jamaica's first marine protected area. A 1974 Beach Control Order first fenced off a small protected zone at Doctor's Cave; the park itself was formally created by the Marine Park Order of August 1992, covering roughly 15 square kilometers of coastline between Sangster International Airport and the Great River. Jamaica's Natural Resources Conservation Authority (NRCA) has co-managed the park with the Montego Bay Marine Park Trust since 1996, and a dedicated fish sanctuary at Airport Point was formally designated under the Fisheries Act in June 2009. None of that legal protection changes the sand's actual makeup: predictive geological modeling (GloPrSM v1.0.0) estimates the composition at roughly 68% quartz, 3% feldspar, and 29% lithic grains, a mixed regime rather than the uniform white coral sand the marketing photography suggests.

## Two Events That Reset the Record

Two recent events sit outside the century-old founding story but now belong to any honest account of the place. In March 2019, Gloucester Avenue, the strip the beach fronts, was renamed Jimmy Cliff Boulevard, honoring the reggae musician at a ceremony at Old Hospital Park on 28 March 2019, in the parish of his birth, where Tessanne Chin and other artists performed his songs in his honor as he received a citation, joined by Culture Minister Olivia Grange. It was a rare moment where the strip's branding pointed at Jamaican culture rather than a colonial-era doctor.

Then, on October 28, 2025, Hurricane Melissa made landfall in Jamaica as a Category 5 storm, the costliest hurricane in the country's recorded history at an estimated $12.2 billion in damage, with 45 deaths nationally and roughly 150,000 structures damaged. Sangster International Airport, about 2.8 km from Doctor's Cave by road (roughly 5 minutes), took the worst structural damage of Jamaica's three international airports; its terminal roof was torn open and passenger flights were suspended before gradually resuming. Seven weeks later, on December 15, 2025, Jamaica's tourism minister opened the winter season by declaring the sector only 71 percent ready. Nine months on, the beach itself reads as recovered in current condition reports, and the site's own storm-exposure data, 15 tropical cyclone passages recorded near this coastline in 50 years, with a maximum of Category 5, shows that Melissa was an extreme instance of a documented pattern, not an outlier event.

## What the History Actually Explains

The through-line from a sanatorium to a surgeon's testimonial to a marine park to a street renaming to a Category 5 hurricane is that Doctor's Cave has never really been about the water quality on any given day. It has been about who gets to decide the water means something, and who gets asked to pay for the privilege of finding out. The doctor who founded it is a documented historical figure; the doctor who made it famous made a claim that was never tested; the club that runs it today still charges at the gate, and the storm that just tested the whole coastline is recent enough that it belongs in the present tense, not the past.
