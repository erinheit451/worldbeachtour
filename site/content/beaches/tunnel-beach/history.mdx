# The Tunnel: A Rich Man's Shortcut, Not a Grief Memorial

Every write-up of Tunnel Beach mentions the tunnel before the beach. That ordering is correct — the beach itself is a small, tide-dependent cove that would draw little notice on its own. What makes the site is the passage cut into the cliff above it: 72 steps of hand-tool-marked Caversham Sandstone, dim, low-ceilinged, dropping walkers through rock to reach the sand. The tunnel has its own history, separate from the beach's, and much of what circulates about it locally does not hold up.

## Who Actually Cut the Rock

The tunnel was cut in the 1870s by John Cargill (1821–1898), a Dunedin-area politician and pastoralist. Cargill sat as MP for Dunedin Country from 1853 to 1858, then for the Bruce electorate from 1866 to 1870. His money came from sheep: he ran roughly 55,000 merino on the Teviot run in South Otago, one of the large pastoral holdings carved out of the province in the decades after his father, Captain William Cargill, led the Otago Association's settlers ashore at Dunedin aboard the *John Wickliffe* on 23 March 1848. William went on to serve as Otago Province's first Superintendent, from 1853 to 1859. The family, in other words, was present at the province's founding and profited from it for two generations.

John Cargill's tunnel was a private-access project. His family held land above the cliffs south of the young settlement, at a time when this whole stretch of coast was carved up into large runs rather than public reserve. Wanting a direct route down to the beach below rather than a long walk around, he had a passage hand-cut through the sandstone. That is the entire documented motive: convenience for a wealthy landowner, funded by wool money, executed with hand tools sometime in the 1870s — no source pins the project to a specific year.

Cargill did not stay in Otago. He left New Zealand later in life and died on 2 January 1898 at Okanagan, British Columbia, on the other side of the Pacific from the tunnel that still bears his name locally.

## The Legend That Replaced the Facts

A different story circulates alongside the documented one: that Cargill built the tunnel to give his daughters private access to the beach, and that one of them (some versions say two, or on her sixteenth birthday) drowned there. It is a better story than "a rich man wanted a shortcut." It has stakes, grief, a reason to care. It also has no supporting evidence. Wikipedia states plainly that there is no truth to the drowning story, and the Dunedin Family History Group, drawing on records held by Dunedin Public Libraries (Recollect record 200993), traced all of John and Edward Bowes Cargill's daughters through newspapers, death certificates, and burial records — none drowned.

The persistence of the legend over the plainer, documented explanation says something about how tourist sites acquire meaning. "Devoted father builds his daughters a private stairway to the sea, and one drowns using it" is a story a guide can tell in thirty seconds that makes a strange piece of infrastructure feel emotionally load-bearing. "Wealthy 19th-century sheep baron with a seat in Parliament wanted easier access to his private beach" is accurate, and also a story about land, class, and colonial-era wealth concentration in Otago — less comfortable, more historically useful, and considerably less quotable on a plaque.

## What Actually Threatens the Tunnel Now

The tunnel and the track above it face a real, dated threat that has nothing to do with folklore: the cliffs themselves. In October 2024, a single severe-weather event triggered at least ten large slips along the 1.3-kilometer Tunnel Beach track, forcing a full closure. Repair work and geotechnical assessment kept the site shut for thirteen months. The Department of Conservation reopened the track on Friday, 14 November 2025, timed for the New Zealand summer, with Coastal Otago operations manager Gabe Davies describing the initial phase of work as focused on making the track safe again.

That closure is the more instructive parallel to draw against the legend. The tunnel's actual risk profile — unstable Miocene sandstone cliffs, a track that DOC itself calls strenuous past the first lookout, dangerous currents and waves that DOC warns make the cove unsafe at high tide and in rough seas — is on the public record with names, dates, and job titles attached. None of it required inventing a drowning. The cliffs that failed in 2024 are made of the same Caversham Sandstone Cargill's laborers cut through by hand roughly 150 years earlier; the geology that let him carve a shortcut is the same geology that periodically closes it.

## Public Land, Private Origin

The tunnel opened to the public in 1983, when concrete steps were added to what had been a bare, carved slope; DOC manages the reserve today. That date matters as much as the 1870s carving: for roughly a century, this was private or semi-private land, access controlled by whoever held the title above the cliffs. Its conversion to public reserve, managed track, and posted safety advisories is a 20th-century administrative story layered on top of a 19th-century wealth story — and neither one needed a dead child to be worth telling.
