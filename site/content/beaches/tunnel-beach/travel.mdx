# Tunnel Beach Travel Guide

Tunnel Beach sits on the coast southwest of central Dunedin, New Zealand — not on the Otago Peninsula, which is the arm of land northeast across Otago Harbour. What gets visitors there is a hand-carved tunnel through sandstone cliffs, cut in the 1870s for one family's private use and opened to the public in 1983. This is a short walk to a small, tidal cove with dangerous currents and waves and a genuinely steep track, not a long-stay beach destination.

## Getting There

<DataCard label="Nearest city" value="Dunedin" unit="7.5 km road / 6.2 km straight-line" />
<DataCard label="Nearest airport" value="Dunedin International (DUD)" unit="20.3 km straight-line" />

**By car.** The drive from central Dunedin runs southwest via Blackhead Road onto Tunnel Beach Road, about 7.5 km and 15–20 minutes, ending at a gated Department of Conservation car park. Parking is free; the gate locks at posted hours (8am–9pm from September to March, 9am–5pm from April to August), and vehicles left inside after closing risk being impounded.

**By air.** The dataset's straight-line distance from Tunnel Beach to Dunedin International Airport (DUD) is 20.3 km — that figure is not a road distance. Dunedin Airport's own site states the airport is 27 km south of the city and to allow 30 minutes to drive between the city and the airport. Tunnel Beach itself is a further 7.5 km southwest of central Dunedin, on the same side of the city as the airport, not the opposite side, so the airport-to-beach road route (via SH1 through Mosgiel, or via Brighton) runs roughly 30–35 km and 30–40 minutes — confirm with a route planner before relying on it.

**By public transport.** There is no direct bus service to the Tunnel Beach car park; getting there without a car means a taxi or rideshare from Dunedin.

## The Walk Itself

<DataCard label="Track distance" value="2.6 km return" unit="~150 m descent, trailhead to sea" />

From the car park, the track passes five named clifftop lookouts ("nests") before reaching the tunnel. The Department of Conservation describes the section beyond the first lookout as strenuous — steep, with narrow stretches, and slippery when wet. The tunnel itself is 72 steps of hand-cut, hand-tool-marked stone with dim natural light and no handrail. Good grip footwear matters more here than at most beach walks; this is a coastal track with real elevation change, not a boardwalk.

## Tide and Timing

The cove below the tunnel is small and tidal. At high tide, the usable sand shrinks and waves can reach closer to the cliff base; the Department of Conservation posts no tide table on site, so checking one before descending is a genuine planning step, not a suggestion. Late afternoon puts direct light down the tunnel shaft and onto the sea arch — in summer the 9pm gate closing allows a full sunset visit; in winter the 5pm closing cuts that off well before golden hour reaches the water.

## Safety

DOC warns of dangerous currents and waves at Tunnel Beach, calls the cove unsafe at high tide and in rough seas, and notes it is not patrolled — there is no lifeguard and no reliable cell reception in the cove. DOC also warns that the site has high, unfenced cliffs with steep drops and unstable edges, and that thieves may target cars parked at the Tunnel Beach car park, so take valuables with you. New Zealand fur seals and sea lions haul out on the rocks below the cliffs; DOC's posted rule is to stay at least 20 meters back.

**The track was closed for thirteen months.** In October 2024, a single storm triggered at least ten large slips along the 1.3-kilometer track, closing it entirely. Repairs and geotechnical assessment kept the site shut until the Department of Conservation reopened it on 14 November 2025. The cliffs are Caversham Sandstone, a relatively soft Miocene marine deposit — the same rock that made a hand-carved tunnel possible in the 1870s is prone to slipping after heavy rain now. Check current DOC alerts before visiting, since another severe-weather event on this stretch of coast could close the track again.

## Costs and Facilities

Access is free — no parking fee, no entry fee. There is nothing to buy on site: no kiosk, no vendor, no water refill point, no rubbish bin. Bring your own water and carry out what you carry in. The nearest food and services are in St Clair, roughly 2.3 km northeast as the crow flies (the road route via Blackhead Road is longer), or in central Dunedin itself.

## Where to Stay

Tunnel Beach has no accommodation of its own; it is a DOC reserve with a car park and a track, nothing more. Central Dunedin, about 7.5 km northeast by road, has the region's full range of hotels, motels, and hostels and is the practical base for a visit.
