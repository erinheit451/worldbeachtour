# The Nortada: Why Guincho Became Portugal's Home of Wave-Sailing

Praia do Guincho is a wind beach before it is anything else. The athletes who fill it in July, the currents that keep families out of the water, and the dune marching inland behind it all trace back to one fact: the summer nortada, an Atlantic north wind that Guincho catches harder than almost anywhere on the Portuguese coast.

## What the wind is

The nortada is the prevailing north-to-northwest wind of the Portuguese west coast in summer. It is driven by two pressure features working together: the semi-permanent Azores High sitting out in the Atlantic, and the thermal low that forms over the heated interior of the Iberian Peninsula. The gradient between them pulls air southward down the coast, day after day, through June, July, and August.

Most of the coast feels this wind. Guincho feels it more because of where it sits. The Serra de Sintra rises directly behind the beach, and the coastline turns sharply at Cabo da Roca, the westernmost point of mainland Europe, just to the north. Air compresses and accelerates as it rounds that high ground and funnels onto this stretch of open sand. The effect is a wind that blows reliably and strong here on afternoons when other coasts are calm.

## Why the sport found it

Wave-sailing needs two things at the same time, and they rarely coincide. It needs ocean swell to ride, and it needs wind to sail on. Most powerful surf coasts get their biggest swell in winter, when the local wind is unreliable and often blowing the wrong direction. Most reliably windy places have no real swell. Guincho delivers both together, and it delivers the wind in the summer months, when a contest can be scheduled and a crowd can stand on the beach to watch.

That is why the sport arrived early. Guincho hosted Portugal's first major international windsurfing event in the summer of 1986, drawing riders including Robby Naish. Through the 1990s and 2000s it was a fixture on the Professional Windsurfers Association (PWA) World Cup wave tour; the PWA World Cup Guincho Wave Contest of 15-19 June 2005 offered €55,000 in prize money. In 2025 organizers announced the Guincho Wave Classic, a bid to return tour-level wave-sailing to the beach. The nortada, not the view, is what put Guincho on the world tour.

## The waves underneath the wind

The reanalysis wave record for this stretch of coast, from Open-Meteo drawing on the ERA5 ocean model, puts the annual mean significant wave height near 2.29 metres. The biggest month is March at about 3.34 metres; the calmest is July at about 1.57 metres; the peak modelled day reaches 5.68 metres. The typical swell period runs around 10 seconds, and the beach breaks over a shifting sand bottom that throws both left and right-breaking waves.

The seasons split the disciplines. Winter brings easterly offshore winds and larger swells, the surfer's window. The summer nortada, blowing side-shore to onshore over smaller northwest swell, is the classic wave-sailing setup. In both modes Guincho rewards riders who already know the beach and punishes those who don't.

## The same wind that keeps you out of the water

The exposure that feeds the wind and swell is exactly what makes Guincho dangerous to swim. Its own references list strong currents, a powerful shore break, and rapidly changing wind, and specifically warn inexperienced swimmers and families with young children away from the water. Guincho carries a Blue Flag, but that rates facilities and water quality, not calm. This is the honest core of the beach: the property that makes it worth a page is the property that makes it unsafe for the thing most people go to a beach to do.

## The wind that moves the land

Behind the sand, the same wind does slower work. Guincho anchors the seaward edge of the Guincho-Cresmina-Oitavos dune system, 66 hectares of mobile sand that the northwest wind pushes inland at roughly 10 metres a year, carrying grains about 5 km south over the low rocky ground near Cabo Raso before they return to the sea near Guia. The dune is a transport belt powered by the same air the sailors ride. To keep foot traffic from breaking it apart, Cascais and Portugal's nature-conservation institute opened a dune park in 2011 with close to 2 km of raised boardwalk.

Guincho's singular claim is the combination and the reliability: a hard, dependable summer wind on an unsheltered Atlantic beach within an hour of Lisbon, arriving in exactly the months a sport can use it. That is what drew the world tour, what builds the dune, and what the camera caught in 1969, when the opening of On Her Majesty's Secret Service filmed George Lazenby's Bond wading into this same surf. The wind is the beach.
