Shankhumugham Beach is an urban beach on the western side of Thiruvananthapuram, the capital of Kerala, in South India, about 8 km from the city center. It sits close enough to Thiruvananthapuram International Airport that part of the Indian Air Force's Southern Air Command, along with a section of the main airport itself, occupies the ground beside it. The beach does not have a single identity so much as several overlapping ones: a commuter landmark next to the airport, a ceremonial ground for one of the city's oldest temple traditions, a public park with a stalled renovation history, and, increasingly, a coastline being measured for how fast it is disappearing.

The beach's best-known feature is the Sagarakanyaka, a stone mermaid sculpture more than 35 metres long, carved by the sculptor Kanayi Kunhiraman, a former student of the Chennai College of Arts. It is the image most associated with Shankhumugham and the reason many visitors come specifically to this stretch of coast rather than to Kerala's more famous swimming beaches nearby. Alongside it sits the Jawaharlal Nehru Park of Traffic Signs for Children — also recorded as the Chacha Nehru Traffic Training Park — a children's road-safety park that was last properly renovated in 2010 and that a 2019 report found still closed a year into a subsequent renovation lease.

Shankhumugham's deeper identity is religious. The beach is regarded as the arattukadavu, the ritual bathing ghat, of Sri Ananthapadmanabhan, the presiding deity of the city, and thousands of Hindus perform the ancestor-offering rite bali tharpanam here on occasions such as Karkidaka Vavu. It is also the district's prime site for Ganesh idol immersion during Ganesh Chaturthi. The beach's grandest ceremonial role comes twice a year, during the Painkuni and Alpashi festivals, when Thiruvananthapuram airport closes so that the titular Maharaja of Travancore can personally lead the Arattu procession — sword in hand, escorted by armed guards, temple authorities, and mounted police — to bathe the idols of Padmanabhaswamy, Narasimha, and Krishna at the beach's old stone Aarattu Mandapam. The Shankhumugham Devi Temple, standing on the beach grounds and counted among the 108 important Durga temples of ancient Kerala, draws its largest crowds on Tuesdays, Fridays, full-moon days, and the first day of each Malayalam month.

Who goes to Shankhumugham depends on the day. On an ordinary afternoon it draws families, photographers, and travelers connecting through the adjacent airport, there for the sculpture, the open sea view, and the sunset. On ritual days it draws devotees by the thousand, arriving for bali tharpanam or for the Ganesh immersion procession. Twice a year it draws an armed royal procession and the crowds that follow it. Kerala Police patrol the beach and lifeguards are stationed on the sand, though this research found no confirmed entry fee, posted hours, or detailed swimming-safety guidance beyond that patrol presence.

What connects all of this to the beach's coastline is erosion, which has visibly reduced the beach's area over time and, by a 2022 study's measurement, continues at 4.73 meters a year — serious enough that it closed a stretch of the beach's own access road in 2021.
