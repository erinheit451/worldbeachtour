# The Road the Sea Took: Erosion at Shankhumugham Beach

Shankhumugham Beach sits on ground that is measurably shrinking. A 2022 study found the coastline here eroding at 4.73 meters a year, and Wikipedia's own entry on the beach cites a 2023 headline warning that Thiruvananthapuram's famed beaches, this one included, could vanish within five years. That is not an abstraction for a future decade. By September 2021, erosion had already closed a 500-metre stretch of the road connecting the beach to the airport, and the sea had already taken the beach's benches and walkways before it reached the asphalt.

## What the Numbers Say

The erosion figure most directly attached to Shankhumugham is the 2022 study's 4.73 meters a year. That number describes the region including this beach, not a single fixed point, but it is the only measured rate in the record, and it sits behind everything else that has happened here since. Wikipedia's citation of a 2023 warning that the city's beaches could vanish within five years is a headline, not a study, and this research did not independently verify the article behind it — but it is consistent with what had already happened on the ground two years earlier, in 2021, when a major part of the beach had, in a reporter's words, "nearly disappeared."

Before the road went, smaller things went first. The beach's benches and walkways were the earliest amenities the sea took, washed away in high tides well before the erosion reached anything as substantial as a paved road. That sequence — furniture, then walkways, then the road itself — is the shape erosion took here, and it is the shape that makes the 2022 rate feel less like a statistic and more like a schedule.

## The Road That Erosion Closed

The clearest, most concrete consequence in this record is the closure of the Shankumugham-airport road. As of a September 2021 report, police had shut a 500-metre stretch of it roughly five months earlier, after the sea destroyed it. That stretch mattered because it was the easiest route from most of the city to the airport's domestic terminal — not a scenic backroad, but a primary access route to Thiruvananthapuram International Airport, one of Kerala's busiest gateways and a facility significant enough to sit beside a military area of the Indian Air Force's Southern Air Command.

With the road gone, passengers arriving at or leaving the airport by that route were left to walk the broken stretch on foot, carrying their own luggage over what used to be pavement. A Public Works Department official quoted at the time made clear this was not a one-time incident to be patched over: rebuilding the road without a scientific solution, the official said, would only see it erode again within months. This research found no later source confirming whether a scientific fix, or any repair at all, was ever carried out. The road's current status, as of this writing, is unconfirmed.

## An Informal Economy

Erosion of this kind does not just remove infrastructure; it displaces the people who depended on it, and in Shankhumugham's case it produced a small, specific, documented economy. An auto driver named Jesudas began carrying stranded passengers' luggage across the broken stretch of road, charging around Rs 100 a bag for the service. He did not invent a business; he filled a gap the erosion had created, on a road with no other way through for people carrying suitcases.

A fisherwoman named Alphonsa tells a related but separate story. Her fish stall was destroyed by the coastal erosion directly, not merely inconvenienced by the road closure, and she responded by selling her catch sitting on the road itself, without a stall. Between Jesudas and Alphonsa, the record shows two people whose livelihoods were reorganized, in real time, by a coastline retreating under them — not as background detail to a beach profile, but as the direct, human-scale consequence of the erosion rate cited above.

## The Public Response, Unconfirmed

Public investment in this stretch of coast has a paper trail that stops well short of resolution. In May 2019, officials described a Kerala State Planning Board–linked masterplan to develop the beachfront in three stages, at an estimated cost of Rs 14.6 crore, with everything scheduled for completion by March 31, 2020. That target arrived while a much smaller project on the same ground — the Chacha Nehru Traffic Training Park, last properly renovated in 2010 — sat closed through another summer vacation, a full year into a renovation lease the District Tourism Promotion Council had signed for it.

Neither the masterplan's completion nor the traffic park's reopening is confirmed by any later source available to this research. What is confirmed is the sequence: an ambitious plan announced in 2019, against a backdrop of a smaller, already-stalled renovation, on a coastline that would go on to lose a section of its access road to erosion within roughly two years, and that a study would measure eroding at 4.73 meters a year within roughly three.

## What This Means for a Beach That Still Draws Crowds

None of this has emptied Shankhumugham. Kerala Police still patrol it, lifeguards are still stationed on the sand, and the Sagarakanyaka mermaid sculpture — more than 35 metres of stone carved by Kanayi Kunhiraman — still anchors the beach as a destination. The Shankhumugham Devi Temple, one of 108 important Durga temples of ancient Kerala, still fills with devotees on set days, and the airport beside the beach still closes twice a year for a royal procession that has outlasted the monarchy it belongs to.

But a visitor arriving today with an older description of the beach — one written before September 2021, or before the 2022 erosion study — cannot assume the geography in that description still holds. The benches, the walkways, and a 500-metre stretch of road are all documented as gone at some point in this record, and nothing in the sources read for this page confirms they have been restored. Shankhumugham's erosion is not a side note to its identity as a landmark. It is the reason that identity now comes with an expiration date attached, measured, in the most literal sense, in meters per year.
