Cable Beach is a 22-kilometer stretch of white sand on the eastern Indian Ocean, and also the name of the suburb built up around it, in the Shire of Broome, Western Australia. The beach sits about 4 km west of the Broome central business district, on the remote Kimberley coast in the state's far north. The suburb itself had a population of 5,436 at the 2016 census, the most recent figure on record. It is not a resort development built around the beach so much as a working Broome neighbourhood that happens to front one of the country's best-known stretches of sand.

The name comes from history, not marketing: a telegraph cable was laid between Broome and Java in 1889, and the beach was named for it. That gives Cable Beach a longer institutional history than its current tourism-postcard reputation suggests — it was a piece of colonial-era communications infrastructure before it was a sunset backdrop.

Geographically, the beach runs as one long, continuous frontage rather than a series of separate coves, which is part of what makes it legible at a glance: sand, ocean, and very little else breaking up the view for most of its length. At the extreme southern end sits Gantheaume Point, home to a 1905 lighthouse and a vantage point for watching dolphins and migrating whales offshore. Adjacent to the beach's southern section is Minyirr Park, a coastal reserve with walking trails behind red ochre dunes that protects the traditional land of the Yawuru people.

Who actually goes to Cable Beach breaks down into a few overlapping groups. There are the camel-ride tourists, who come specifically for the sunrise and sunset rides that have become the beach's signature activity. There are photographers timing their visit around the Staircase to the Moon, a phenomenon in which a full moon rising over the exposed mudflats of nearby Roebuck Bay creates the illusion of a staircase reaching toward it — it happens on a handful of nights a month between March and October, and sourcing disagrees on whether that is two, three, or a flat three nights a month. There are Broome locals and dry-season visitors using the patrolled swimming section near the surf club. And there is a much smaller, much older group: naturists who have used a stretch called "north of the rocks" since the 1960s, a tradition now down to a handful of regulars sharing the same ground with the camel operators — the full story of that squeeze is in the culture guide.

None of these groups is hypothetical, and none of them has the beach to itself. Cable Beach's 22 kilometers are wide enough that a camel train, a family swimming near the surf club flags, and a photographer waiting on a lunar tide can all be present at once without crowding each other directly — the beach's scale is doing real work here, not just its reputation. What that scale cannot do is keep every use of the beach static: the northern sections are reached differently depending on the season and, currently, on an ongoing foreshore redevelopment that has changed how vehicles and pedestrians move along the sand. The travel guide covers those access details; this page is about what the beach is and who it is for on an ordinary day, which is: almost everyone who comes to Broome, for a wide range of different reasons, on the same stretch of sand.
