Cala Salada is a pebble cove on the west coast of Ibiza, in the municipality of Sant Antoni de Portmany, about 4.7 km (2.9 mi) north of the town of Sant Antoni de Portmany by Wikipedia's measure (the Spain Film Commission gives a shorter 4 km for the same stretch). It shares its inlet with a smaller sibling cove, Cala Saladeta, next door — a separate beach, not another name for the same one. The two are physically divided by Casa Rosada, a striking mid-20th-century building; Cala Salada is the pebbly, developed half, with a restaurant, lifeguards, toilets and disabled access, while Cala Saladeta is sandy, has no facilities of its own, and gets very busy in summer.

Sources disagree on how big the beach actually is. Wikidata records Cala Salada at 75 metres long by 20 metres wide. Ibiza Spotlight describes the wider bay as 200 metres long and 5 to 30 metres wide; Barceló gives Cala Salada alone as 200 metres long and about 25 metres wide, depending on the tides. The most likely explanation is that the larger figures describe the combined Salada-Saladeta bay rather than Cala Salada by itself — a distinction most visitor copy doesn't bother to make.

What's actually on the ground: sunbeds, parasols, a restaurant known locally for seafood paella, and showers that have been turned off to save water. The road down ends at a small, shaded car park with 65 spaces and stairs to the sand. Cala Salada also marks the start of the Es Amunts nature reserve, which runs along Ibiza's northern coast up to Sant Carles; the rocky, Posidonia-covered seabed offshore is habitat for species like the ornate wrasse, found at depths of 0 to 50 metres and rated of Least Concern by the IUCN.

Who goes: day-trippers from Sant Antoni de Portmany (4.7 km away by Wikipedia's measure), swimmers drawn to the sheltered cove, and anyone chasing the relatively pine-shaded, less-developed feel that guides like White Ibiza contrast with more built-up parts of the coast. What most of that framing leaves out is how much of the beach's calm is actively managed rather than simply preserved by geography — Sant Antoni's town hall has run a scheduled bus and boat here since 2016 and, since 2023, a separate paid checkpoint that physically gates the access road, citing an extreme wildfire-risk classification. That story, including the professional travel guide that flatly denies the public transport exists, is told in full in the [access-control history deep dive](/beaches/cala-salada/history).
