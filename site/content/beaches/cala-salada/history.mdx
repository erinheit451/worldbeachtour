# The Access Contract: How Cala Salada Got Gated

Cala Salada is a cove on the Spanish island of Ibiza, in the municipality of Sant Antoni de Portmany, about 4.7 km (2.9 mi) north of the town by Wikipedia's measure. It is also, less visibly, the origin point of a piece of Ibiza-wide public policy and the subject of a second, separate, and considerably more expensive access-control program. The history of the two is not the same story, even though visitor guides usually collapse them into one vague idea of "the road is restricted."

## 2016: A Pilot, Not a Ban

The first program was a pilot, not a crackdown. Sant Antoni de Portmany's town hall and the Consell d'Eivissa designed "Playa Conectada," a sustainable-mobility scheme that ran, and still runs, from 1 June to 30 September. Under it, private cars are restricted on the Cala Salada access road between 10:00 and 18:00. In place of driving, the town hall stood up a connecting bus running every 15 minutes in both directions, with a first departure from Sant Antoni at 9:30 and a last departure from the beach at 20:30, plus a port boat between Sant Antoni and Cala Salada. A round-trip boat ticket cost 9 euros for adults and 4.5 euros for children aged 7 to 12 — the earliest fares on record, dating to the program's 2016-17 rollout; no updated 2026-season price list for the bus, boat, or parking turned up in the sources available for this page.

The pilot, in the town hall's own account, "tuvo muy buena acogida" — went down well — and the following year, 2017, it expanded island-wide to 30 beaches. Cala Salada was the test case for a policy that now covers a large share of Ibiza's coast. A separate, later account of the same corridor, from Ibiza Spotlight, describes a minibus, line P7, running every 30 minutes from 9:30 to 20:30 with a park-and-ride stop at the Can Coix sports centre — evidently a refinement of the original bus layered on over time. A February 2017 beach review independently flagged a newly introduced number-34 bus running regularly from Sant Antoni, which the reviewer expected to sharply increase visitor volume to both Cala Salada and Cala Saladeta.

## 2023: A Second, Harder Layer

Seven years after the original pilot, Sant Antoni's town hall added something categorically different: a paid, staffed vehicle checkpoint. Where Playa Conectada worked by substitution — take the bus instead of the car — the new program works by physical control at the road itself. The contract, which began in 2023, is worth 113,176.02 euros a year, VAT included. As reported in 2024, it runs for two years with an option to extend for two more.

The stated justification is specific and not cosmetic: the town hall classifies the Cala Salada/Cala Saladeta access zone as an area of extremely high wildfire risk, and the checkpoint's purpose is to keep the road clear enough for emergency vehicles. In the 2026 season, the service runs daily, Monday to Sunday, from 9:30 to 17:00, without interruption. Motorcycles and bicycles, vehicles for people with reduced mobility, taxis, and local residents or workers are all exempt; every other private car is turned back at the checkpoint during those hours. In 2024, two years before that 2026 reactivation, the town hall also built a signposted pedestrian path, about 500 metres long, connecting the first car park to the beach, so that bathers without cars are not forced to walk the access road on foot alongside whatever traffic remains.

Put the two programs side by side and the picture is of a municipality that has spent close to a decade building layered infrastructure — a substitute bus, a substitute boat, a second minibus line, a dedicated footpath, and now a staffed checkpoint — specifically to manage how people reach one small cove. That is not the behavior of a place relying on natural obscurity to stay quiet. It is active, continuously funded traffic management.

## The Claim That Doesn't Match the Record

Against that record sits a flat contradiction from a professional source. Barceló, a hotel operator that publishes its own Ibiza visitor guide, tells readers there is no public transport that leads to Cala Salada, and suggests taking a taxi from Sant Antoni instead. Barceló also gives a driving figure for context: about 20 km from Ibiza Town, roughly 25 minutes, via the C-731 and PM-812 roads — a real, useful number, but one that sits next to an access claim that is simply wrong.

The town hall has published a bus timetable and boat fares for this exact route since 2016. Ibiza Spotlight, an independent travel-information site, separately and specifically describes a working minibus line (P7) on the same corridor, with its own frequency and park-and-ride details. A 2017 review from a third, unrelated source flags the same corridor's bus service as newly upgraded that year. Three separate sources, spanning nearly a decade, describe public transport that a fourth source says does not exist.

This is not a case of ambiguous or since-changed service — the underlying program has been continuous since 2016. It suggests, at minimum, that one part of the professional travel-content industry is republishing assumptions about "hidden," hard-to-reach beaches without checking the municipal record for a beach it otherwise covers in detail, right down to driving directions and distances.

## What the Record Actually Shows

None of this makes Cala Salada a fabricated escape. The cove is real, the facilities are real — a restaurant, lifeguards, toilets, disabled access — and the Es Amunts nature reserve genuinely begins here and runs up Ibiza's northern coast. What the record shows is that the version of Cala Salada visitors experience, particularly its relative calm against a narrow access road and a small, 65-space car park, is a managed outcome, not an accident of geography. It costs public money: 113,176.02 euros a year for the checkpoint alone, on top of whatever the bus-and-boat program costs to run every summer.

The wildfire-risk classification behind the 2023 checkpoint is the strongest, least sentimental part of the story — it is a safety measure with an operational reason, not a marketing device. But it sits directly underneath a tourism narrative, built by multiple travel brands over multiple years, that markets the same managed cove as untouched. Both things are true at once: Cala Salada is genuinely well run, and it is not what "unspoiled" usually implies.
