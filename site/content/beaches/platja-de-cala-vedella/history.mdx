# The Fifty-Two-Year File: Cala Vedella's Unfinished Urbanización

Cala Vedella looks, from the sand, like a finished place: a Blue Flag cove, lifeguards in summer, 250 parking spaces, two beachfront restaurants carrying the island's own local-produce seal. Behind it sits a residential development that Sant Josep de Sa Talaia has never formally agreed to call complete — a file that has now run for 52 years without a close date.

## The 1973 Plan and Its Missing Paperwork

In 1973, Sant Josep de Sa Talaia approved a partial development plan for Cala Vedella. Under Spanish planning law, a partial plan like this is only the first step: the urbanizing agent responsible for the land — in this case a company called UMSA — is then supposed to complete a set of legal instruments (infrastructure handover, reparcelling, compensation projects) that formally "closes" the plan and regularizes everything built under it. UMSA never finished that paperwork.

That did not stop construction. Homes went up, roads were laid, and a private wastewater treatment plant was built to serve the urbanización. Decades passed with the underlying legal file still open. In a filing dated 30 April 2025, the development's owners told Sant Josep town hall that Cala Vedella was now more than 90% built out, with water, street lighting, sewerage, refuse collection, and electricity all fully operating and in use — and asked the town to finally take the urbanización over. Counted from the 1973 approval, that request landed 52 years into the file's life.

## A Building Freeze Since 2007

The unfinished status has had a direct, ongoing effect on development at the margins. Since 2007, Sant Josep has refused to process major building licenses for the roughly one-fifth of Cala Vedella's parcels that remain undeveloped, precisely because the urbanización as a whole was never legally closed out. New building at Cala Vedella has effectively meant renegotiating old licenses rather than obtaining new ones.

The clearest case is Port d'es Nebot, on the edge of the development. A 2005 building license, numbered 363/2005, authorized 61 homes there. That plan was later scaled back through negotiation to just 12 luxury villas. Those 12 villas did eventually get built: in January 2021, the crowdfunding platform Urbanitae financed the project with a €650,000 loan backed by 227 individual investors, developed by Azur Estates. The loan's capital and interest were repaid in full by April 2022, with a final return of 9.1%. Private capital could still move through the parts of Cala Vedella with a settled license — it was the underlying urbanización, as a whole, that stayed unresolved.

## The Treatment Plant the Town Couldn't Touch

The same regulatory limbo reached into the development's basic infrastructure. Cala Vedella's wastewater treatment plant was never public: it is owned by UMSA, the same urbanizing agent that never closed out the 1973 plan. At a June 2018 council session, officials described the plant as sized roughly 50 years earlier and in need of complete renovation.

Fixing it turned out to be legally awkward. Because the town hall could not formally "receive" infrastructure belonging to an urbanización it had never regularized, it could not simply order UMSA to upgrade the plant either. By April 2019, officials confirmed that promised expansion works would not be ready before that summer. By November 2019, Sant Josep's deputy mayor said the town was studying whether it could expropriate the plant outright — an unusual step, but one that reflected how few ordinary levers the town actually had over a private plant serving an unfinished development. That same October, the town hall separately ordered a halt to an unrelated project, Vadella 24 housing, over unlicensed works, and opened disciplinary proceedings against it. Complaints about water conditions in the area persisted into August 2022, when a political party publicly pressed the town hall for answers referencing the plant.

## A Criminal Investigation Runs Alongside It

The Cala Vedella file has not stayed purely administrative. It has run in parallel with a SEPRONA investigation — carried out by the Guardia Civil's environmental-crimes unit — into possible offenses against territorial planning. That investigation led to the detention of a former Sant Josep mayor, Ángel Luis Guerrero. The precise relationship between the SEPRONA case and the urbanización's stalled paperwork is not something the sources spell out in full, but both threads sit inside the same long-running Cala Vedella file, and both were still live when owners filed their May 2025 request.

That petition landed during a broader planning moment for the town: Sant Josep was simultaneously running a public participation process for a new general urban plan, the PGOU, which closed with 241 submitted contributions. Cala Vedella's status is one of the open questions that plan will eventually have to settle, one way or another — the sources available do not say when, or how.

## What Fifty-Two Years Looks Like at Beach Level

None of this changes what a visitor actually encounters. The cove is still rated one of Ibiza's Blue Flag beaches, still has lifeguards on duty in summer, still has 250 parking spaces and two restaurants carrying the Sabors d'Eivissa seal. The dispute lives in council minutes and press filings, not on the sand.

But the gap between the two is real. A development that is, by its own owners' account, over 90% finished has spent more than half a century without a legal file to match — its infrastructure privately owned, its remaining parcels frozen since 2007, its treatment plant too legally tangled to simply repair, and its history running alongside a criminal investigation into the offices meant to regulate it. Cala Vedella is a working beach. It is also an open file.
