# Playa Negra: A Reef Break in an Active Seismic Gap

Playa Negra is a black-sand beach on the Pacific coast of Costa Rica's Nicoya Peninsula, in Guanacaste Province, roughly 27.9 kilometers by straight line from the canton seat of Santa Cruz. The name is simply descriptive — Black Beach, for the dark, mineral-heavy sand this stretch of coast carries. The nearest settlement is Los Pargos, a village that exists because of the surf break rather than the other way around. Public coordinates for "Playa Negra, Costa Rica" (10°11′38″N 85°49′47″W) sit within about 140 meters of this dataset's centroid, so there's no naming mismatch to flag here: this is the well-known Guanacaste surf break, distinct from another black-sand beach of the same name near Puerto Viejo on Costa Rica's Caribbean coast.

## What's Actually Here

The defining feature is the wave, not the sand. Playa Negra breaks right over a rock-reef bottom, with a channel that lets surfers paddle out rather than fight through the break — a description drawn from the beach's own public record. Reanalysis wave data (Open-Meteo Marine, ERA5, 2023–2024) puts the annual mean wave height at 1.33 meters, with June averaging 1.51 meters and March, the calmest month, averaging 1.12 meters. The typical wave period runs around 11.9 seconds, and the model's own character line for this stretch of ocean is blunt: "Exposed — real surf, and rip current to respect."

The sand itself is predicted (not directly sampled) as a lithic-rich volcanic-terrane composition — roughly 38% quartz, 7% feldspar, and 55% lithic rock fragments, per the GloPrSM v1.0.0 global sand model — consistent with the dark volcanic geology common along this coast.

## The Earthquake in the Background

This beach sits inside Santa Cruz canton, the same administrative area struck hardest by the magnitude-7.6 Nicoya Peninsula earthquake of September 5, 2012, centered about 11 kilometers southeast of Nicoya town at a depth of 40.2 kilometers. That earthquake ruptured a segment seismologists had monitored for decades as a mature "seismic gap," overdue since a magnitude-7.7 predecessor in 1950. Damage across the region totaled an estimated $44.8 million, with at least 169 houses damaged and two deaths. Playa Negra's own record credits that earthquake with reshaping the reef into a better wave — a claim that sits on a page Wikipedia's own editors flag as citing no sources. The earthquake is real and well documented; the effect on this specific reef is not independently confirmed.

## Access Doesn't Match the Map

The dataset's nearest airport by straight-line distance is Nosara (NOB), a small domestic-only airstrip 31.1 kilometers away that handled 7,350 passengers in all of 2014. Most travelers who fly in for this beach actually use Liberia's Daniel Oduber Quirós International Airport (LIR), farther away by straight line but the only practical international gateway, moving close to two million passengers a year as of 2024. The road into Los Pargos, the village nearest the beach, remains unpaved — culverts and bridges have been added over time, and the road itself got seven meters of rock base added in 2010 — even as the village has fiber-optic internet rated to 200 megabits per second and restaurants serving Peruvian, French, and Mexican food.

**Surf** — The right-hand reef break, the wave data, the seismic-gap geology underneath it, and the unresolved claim about what the 2012 earthquake did to the wave are covered in depth in the [surf deep-dive](/beaches/playa-negra-6/surf).

Playa Negra rewards specifics over atmosphere: a documented earthquake in a documented seismic gap, a wave dataset that calls the surf "real" and warns about rip current, and an access record — road, airport, internet — that doesn't match the beach's reputation for remoteness.
